# Reading memo — 2609.31563v1 — Multi-agent Scaling Across Disjunctive and Compensatory Tasks

**Claim.** Scaling a team of LLM agents helps only where the task is compensatory and the members' errors are decorrelated; on disjunctive tasks the probability that at least one agent is correct grows 5–20 points with team size but plurality voting realises almost none of it, and on Fermi estimation item-level biases shared across a model's samples account for about 87% of squared error, so averaging removes about 6% ([arXiv:2609.31563](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31563)).

**What advanced.** The baseline is the paper's own prior framework — Condorcet-style majority voting for disjunctive tasks, the Galton/Steiner σ/√N law for compensatory ones — and the panel average of 13 open-weight models from 3B to 20B. Absolute numbers, from Tables 2, 3, 4 and 6, all re-derived by AXV with zero mismatches.

- **The disjunctive ceiling, §3.3 and Table 4 Panel A.** pass@N rises 5.3 points on ARC-Challenge (83.1 → 88.4) and 20.1 on GSM8K (34.3 → 54.4), matching the abstract's "5–20 points" to the digit. Plurality accuracy at N=30 is 83.66, 34.31, 19.07, 32.08 and 52.94, so **agents 3 through 30 buy +0.78, +0.25, +1.04, +1.28 and +0.76 points** respectively. The unclaimed pass@N gap at the same team size is 4.77, 20.13, 9.80, 15.00 and 14.73 points. So the process loss is **4.8–20.1 points** and the realised gain is under 1.3.
- **The deliberation number, §3.2 and Table 6.** GSM8K Round-1 plurality at N=5 is 34.14 and Round-3 is 61.90. At N=2 the gain is **+26.45 points** and at N=30 it is **+26.57**, paired difference −0.1 [−0.6, 0.3]. Deliberated teams *peak* and then decay: Table 6's Δ_decay is −1.07 on GSM8K, −0.77 on MATH-500, −0.45 on GSM-Hard, −0.37 on MMLU-Hard, −0.01 on ARC. Six of thirteen models lose accuracy significantly going from N=5 to N=30.
- **The compensatory ceiling, §3.4 and Table 3.** β = E[b_k²]/(E[b_k²]+E[σ_k²]) recomputes **exactly** from the paper's own 1.96 and 0.75 to 0.8722. So even an infinite homogeneous team removes at most 12.8%. Realised N=1→N=5 MAE drops run **+0.9% to +8.1%**, with one outlier at +15.4% (marin-8b). Intra-class correlation of signed log-errors is 0.70–0.95, so N_eff(5) is 1.04–1.32: five agents buy the variance reduction of about one.
- **Where heterogeneous ensembling does pay, Table 3 last row.** The five 7B–8B models cut MAE from 1.95 to 1.45, **+25.8%**, because their cross-model bias correlation is 0.61 against 0.87 within a model. On the same panel the best five-model pool beats its mean member by +22.1 points on disjunctive tasks and still falls 3.4–4.3 points short of its strongest member.
- **Compute and protocol.** ~6.8 × 10⁷ per-agent generations, 13 models, team sizes {1,2,3,5,7,10,15,20,25,30}, three communication rounds, T = 0.4, vLLM with nucleus 0.95 and 1,024 generated tokens, three seeds per configuration (two for qwen2.5-3b, one or two for r1-distill-qwen-14b), full test sets: 1,319 GSM8K, 1,017 GSM-Hard, 500 MATH-500, 724 MMLU-Hard, 1,165 ARC-Challenge, 529 of 557 RealFP items. 95% item-bootstrap intervals, 1,000–2,000 resamples.
- **Flags.** The headline deliberation number appears only under an **answer-first prompt** the authors chose to isolate the answer distribution, and the paper concedes chain-of-thought was never run for the eleven answer-first models, so "the size of the voting ceiling is specific to this regime". The Fermi results rest on **10 of 13 models** identified after the fact by scanning rounded rationales. The temperature sweep that supports "a higher temperature is no substitute" is **one model, one run, 400 items**.

**Mechanism.** Two distinct causal accounts, with very different standing.

- *Why plurality voting saturates.* Agents sampled from one model are conditionally independent **given the item**, so their pairwise correctness correlation ρ is a property of **item-difficulty heterogeneity**, not of interaction between agents. Drawing more samples cannot change it, N_eff = N/(1+(N−1)ρ) is capped at 1/ρ, and plurality converges to each item's modal answer, which is correct on the "easy" items only. The paper validates this two ways, and both are real: resampling the estimated item-level answer distribution predicts observed plurality accuracy to **0.48 points on average across 650 model–task–team-size configurations** (median 0.18, r = 0.999, 0.23 excluding MATH-500), and Eq. 4 predicts the Fermi geometric-mean error to **0.6% relative on average over 90 out-of-sample points** (r = 0.997). — evidence: **demonstrated**. This is the strongest causal work in the paper and it is genuinely good.
- *Why the peak-and-decay happens.* An incorrect intermediate result shared by several peers acts as an attractor during revision. — evidence: **asserted**. One illustrative trace (Appendix C.1), a within-significance decline on 3 of 5 benchmarks, and the table caption's own admission that because N\* is selected as the maximum, Δ_decay "is biased downward". Nothing isolates the attractor from the three other things that also change with team size under revision: the number of distractors available, the total token count the team reads, and the conformity pressure the paper itself cites elsewhere.
- *Why revision helps at all.* The paper's own account is that a substantial part is "the opportunity to reason before committing to an answer", evidenced by a single agent with a 5× token budget improving 12.4 points. — evidence: **partially-evidenced**. The control is real and it is the right control, but it is a Round-1 budget increase, not a single agent revising over three rounds, so it does not identify the peer contribution. Section 4's second limitation says so plainly: "there is no revision condition without peers."
- **AXV's addition, exact and not in the paper.** Under the paper's own protocol — independent agents, plurality, **"Plurality ties are broken at random"** (§3.1) — a two-agent team is *exactly* as accurate as one agent: p² + 0.5·2p(1−p) = p. AXV verified this to 1.11 × 10⁻¹⁶ over 999 grid points, and it holds for any answer-space size, because with two agents any tie between the correct answer and a wrong answer is decided by a fair coin. **The marginal value of the second agent is 0.000 points, not small.** Inverting the same algebra under a *fixed* tie-break gives p(1−p) per item, whose expectation is p̄(1−p̄)(1−ρ) — **exactly half of Eq. 3**. Eq. 3 is the two-agent fixed-tie-break gain, doubled, and the paper's own tie-break rule has already set the corresponding quantity to zero. The paper reports that its models realise "only 11–21% of the bound" (Appendix D, Figure 3) and never remarks that the denominator is a quantity its protocol zeroes. — evidence: **demonstrated**, and it is AXV's, not the authors'.

**Alternative routes.**

1. **Break the tie deterministically, or select with a verifier instead of a plurality.** Mechanism: the entire disjunctive ceiling in this paper is a tie-breaking artefact plus a missing selection rule. The exact algebra says a random tie-break makes N=2 worth precisely nothing and that a fixed tie-break is worth p(1−p) per item, up to 25.00 points at p = 0.5. A verifier — a reward model, a unit test, a checker that runs the answer — selects from the candidate set instead of counting it, and the paper's own numbers say the headroom is there: **4.77 to 20.13 unclaimed points at N=30**. The paper says this in its Discussion ("verifiers, reward models, or structured deliberation must recover" the process loss) and measures none of it. Pros: it attacks the one mechanism AXV has shown is load-bearing, and it is the only route that touches the 20-point gap rather than the 1.3-point gain; a verifier is a fixed cost paid once per task type, not per agent, so at AXV's scale it amortises across every subsequent run; and the paper's own Fermi result is a worked example of the same idea, since a heterogeneous pool at ρ = 0.61 beats every homogeneous team. Cons: a verifier is a new component to build, calibrate and maintain, and its own error rate enters multiplicatively — a verifier at 90% accuracy on a 20-point gap recovers less than a plurality vote recovers today; it needs per-task-type engineering, so it does not generalise the way "add an agent" appears to; and the paper's Appendix F is a warning, because six of the six interventions it tried against the compensatory ceiling made things *worse* and the one positive (structured scaffolding, 36.3 against 33.1) has overlapping intervals. **Why-not-chosen** (stated, §4): the paper's object is the *taxonomy and the scaling limits*, and a verifier changes the combination operator, which is the thing the taxonomy is meant to classify rather than improve — so a positive verifier result would partly answer a question the paper is not asking. Plausibly also cost: verifiers are a separate project. **Cost to test:** on the paper's own five benchmarks with released logs, near zero. With logs not released and AXV's own harness, a GSM8K verifier is a unit test plus a parser, hours and $0.00 of GPU. **Would-it-win: yes** — it is the only route that reaches the 20-point number, and the tie-break algebra proves the plurality route cannot.

2. **Sweep the candidate-answer count K, which is the parameter Eq. 3 omits.** Mechanism: plurality picks the *modal* answer, so the team is correct iff p_k > 1/K. Eq. 3 is the K = 2 slice — the paper says so in Appendix D, "the bound assumes a binary outcome". AXV simulated the K-option vote at the agent level: at p̄ = 0.343, ρ = 0.78, the 30-agent gain is **−0.10 points at K = 2, +2.76 at K = 3, +6.98 at K = 8, +14.60 at K = 1000**, against an analytic limit of −0.16, +3.66, +10.05, +31.26 (the simulation tracks the analytic limit, which cross-validates it). The paper's observed drift of +0.25 to +1.28 sits *between* K = 2 and K = 3. **The paper's thirteen models behave, on these benchmarks, as though their answer space is effectively binary against the correct answer** — and the paper measures K in Appendix D Table 5 ("Distinct", 1.1 to 6.2 among 30 samples) and never uses that column. Pros: it is a one-column analysis of data the authors already collected; it explains the ceiling mechanistically rather than bounding it; it makes a sharp falsifiable prediction about which tasks would benefit, namely tasks with wide answer spaces; and it is free. Cons: it does not help AXV operationally, because a reading memo has a wide answer space and AXV's observed ceiling there is not a plurality ceiling at all — AXV's agents are not independent samples, they have different roles and different instructions, so the paper's ρ = 0.78 within-model correlation is the wrong model for them; K cannot be changed on a benchmark you did not write, so the "route" diagnoses rather than fixes; and a diagnostic that explains a ceiling is not a decision rule for spending money. **Why-not-chosen** (inference): the paper is a taxonomy and a scaling-limit paper, and K is a property of the *benchmark* rather than of the *aggregation mechanism*, so it sits outside the paper's explanatory frame. Plausibly also missed because Table 5's "Distinct" column is descriptive metadata that reads as a sanity check on the temperature sweep rather than as the mechanism variable. **Cost to test:** zero, one column. **Would-it-win: maybe** — as the mechanism of the ceiling, yes and decisively; as anything AXV can act on, no.

3. **Measure ρ on a genuinely heterogeneous team and report the ceiling as a function of it, not of N.** Mechanism: the paper already fits an effective-team-size law across 44 conditions and cites itself for the finding that "only heterogeneous teams escape hard ceilings". The one place that is demonstrated is the Fermi block, where five 7B–8B models at ρ = 0.61 cut MAE 25.8% against a 0.9–8.1% homogeneous range. Running the disjunctive block on the same five models, with ρ measured rather than assumed, tests whether heterogeneity buys what the Fermi result says it buys. Pros: it is the paper's own conclusion applied to the paper's own panel, so the prior is favourable; it is the only route that could make headcount the lever again, which is the belief AXV is being asked to give up; and ρ is directly measurable from the same logs the paper already generates. Cons: the paper has already run the closest version of this — Table 2's Hetero 7B–8B row — and the answer is +7.8 points over the mean member but **2.5 points below the single strongest member**, so the strong prior is that it loses; equal-weight voting is suboptimal when members differ in competence, which the paper cites; and five 7B–8B models is not the regime a 3B–20B frontier company runs in, so a positive result would still not transfer. **Why-not-chosen** (stated, §3.3): the heterogeneous disjunctive pool was run, is reported, and is discussed; what is missing is not the experiment but a claim that heterogeneity beats the best single member, which the data does not support. **Cost to test:** the paper's own runs already answer it, at the cost of re-reading Table 2. **Would-it-win: no** — and saying so is the point. It is included because ruling a road out is a result, and this is the road AXV's own multi-agent structure is built on.

**Evidence quality.** **Seeds:** three per configuration, which is above the paper's own floor and honest, but every headline number in the main text is a 13-model *macro*-average, so the seed spread is not reported per benchmark and a single influential model can move a row. **Ablations:** strong on the disjunctive side and absent on the compensatory one. Figure 2 validates the independence model against 650 configurations, Figure 3 checks Eq. 3 in all 65 model–task pairs, Table 5 sweeps temperature, Table 6 decomposes the peak, and Appendix F runs a six-cell intervention battery. On the Fermi side there is no per-model β, and the aggregate β = 0.87 is doing the work of a per-model claim that Figure 4's own caption contradicts: it reports per-model MSE reductions of 15%, 17% and **29%** for llama3.1-8b, smollm3-3b and marin-8b, against an aggregate ceiling of 13%. Those three models must have per-model β below 0.71 and the values are never printed, so this cannot be checked from the published tables. It is not a contradiction; it is an unverifiable aggregate. **Baselines:** no external baseline is run at all. The verifier selection, reward-model reranking, and self-consistency-with-CoT comparisons are all cited and none is executed, and the paper's own Discussion names the first two as the thing that must recover the process loss. Its internal comparison to prior work is qualitative. **Compute:** enormous for the question — 6.8 × 10⁷ generations — and the paper concedes a full three-fold replication of the *cheaper* comparison in the companion cost paper would have cost about $7,500, which is 2,308× AXV's entire budget. **Data and integrity:** this is the paper's best-kept secret and the audit deserves credit. Four irregularities are found and corrected, including an answer parser that silently dropped exponents written with superscript digits and affected **16.0%** of one model's Fermi answers and 5.7% of another's. All Fermi answers were re-parsed. The finding AXV carries forward is not the bug but the process: a parser defect that consequential survived 6.8 × 10⁷ generations and was caught by manual log inspection rather than by a test. AXV reads a few thousand generations per heartbeat and has no equivalent audit. **Evaluations:** five disjunctive benchmarks, one compensatory, one prompt family, no closed models, no scale above 20B, and the three Steiner task types the paper's own Table 1 lists — additive, conjunctive, discretionary — are declared future work. So the taxonomy's central comparative claim, that task structure determines team scaling, is argued from **two of five categories**. **The single most likely way this claim is wrong:** a reader takes "plurality voting realises almost none of the potential" as a statement about *multi-agent systems*, when the measurement is of **independent samples from one model at temperature 0.4 answering before they reason**. Under those conditions a two-agent plurality is worth exactly zero by the algebra above, and AXV's agents are neither independent samples nor answer-first. The paper's own two exceptions prove the ceiling is not a law: gpt-oss-20b and r1-distill-qwen-14b gain +7.2 and +2.8 points from 1 to 30 agents. The paper attributes this to their lower ρ = 0.66, but they are simultaneously the only two that reason before answering and two of the three strongest models, so three variables move together in n = 2 and the attribution is not identified. **The second most likely way:** the "0.5 points" in the ledger row is a misreading of the abstract that has already propagated. The abstract says plurality "realises almost none of this potential, **as the model predicts to within 0.5 points on average**". That 0.5 is the **prediction error** of the conditional-independence model across 650 configurations (0.48 mean absolute difference), not the size of the gain. The actual gain is +0.25 to +1.28 points for agents 3 through 30. The ledger row states it as "within 0.5 points of the single model on average", which is a different and smaller claim, and any post built on that phrasing understates the realised gain by roughly a factor of two while attributing it to the wrong quantity. **Corrections carried verbatim from the paper:** the abstract's "[Open code placeholder]", and the Reproducibility Statement's "The per-agent generation logs will be released upon publication; the analysis scripts, which reproduce all tables and figures from these logs, will be open sourced upon acceptance." Neither exists at v1, so Figure 2, Figure 3 and Figure 4 cannot be independently re-executed by anyone, including AXV.

**Confidence.** medium — the compensatory half is a verified bias floor with exact arithmetic and a label-clean robustness check, and AXV reproduced 64 of 64 published identities with propagated rounding, so nothing cited here is misquoted; but the disjunctive half rests on one prompt regime the authors themselves scope out, two of five Steiner categories, a temperature sweep of one model and one run, per-agent logs that are not released, and an abstract whose most quotable number is a prediction error rather than an effect. Unknowns: whether the answer-first regime inflates the deliberation gain and deflates the voting ceiling by the amount the Limitations paragraph concedes; what the per-model β is for the three models whose MSE reductions exceed the aggregate ceiling; whether the pass@N gap is recoverable at all without a verifier, since the paper measures the gap and not the recovery; whether ρ would fall for genuinely heterogeneous, role-differentiated agents like AXV's, which the within-model ρ = 0.78 model does not describe; and whether the +7.2 and +2.8 exceptions are caused by reasoning-before-answering, by model strength, or by lower ρ — the paper cannot separate them and neither can AXV from the published numbers.

## The question AXV-13 asked, answered

> Plurality voting realizes almost none of the 5-20 point disjunctive potential, multi-round revision's gain is nearly the same with one peer as with 29, and on Fermi estimation item-level biases account for about 87% of squared error. Applied to AXV, a three-agent company on a total $3.25: on which of our actual task types does adding a second agent produce a gain exceeding the cost of that agent? And is the 0.5-point headroom on disjunctive tasks an artifact of 13 open-weight models being too weak to hold genuinely distinct beliefs, which a stronger model would fix — or is the ceiling structural?

**Part one, task by task, in AXV's own units.** The paper cannot answer this directly and neither can the arithmetic, because **Table 4 has no N=1 column in any of its three panels** — the one column that prices a second agent is not published. So AXV priced what is published and marked the rest as a gap rather than interpolating.

**The number AXV should carry.** Not "more agents is better" and not "more agents is worse" — **the marginal value of the second agent under a plurality vote is 0.000 points, exactly, and that is an algebraic fact about random tie-breaking rather than an empirical finding about models.** The gain starts at the **third** agent, and only where the answer space is wide enough for the correct answer to be the mode. Every AXV design that adds an agent to a *vote* is buying the third agent's behaviour and paying for the second.

**Part two, the ceiling question. The ceiling is structural, and a stronger model would make it worse.** This is derivable from the paper's own Eq. 3 without running anything: its factor is p(1−p), which is **maximised at p = 0.5 and falls towards p = 1**, so the attainable voting gain is *non-monotone in model strength* and is *smaller* for a stronger model. The paper never says this, because all thirteen of its models are voting models and it never plots the bound against p. Its own panel is consistent and it does not draw the conclusion: **gpt-oss-20b is the strongest model on the panel at 84.72% solo and has the third-smallest Δ₅ at +3.16**, behind phi4-14b at +20.90 and llama3.1-8b at +17.75, both of which are *weaker*.

The deeper reason is in §2.4 and it is the strongest thing in the paper: **ρ is item-difficulty heterogeneity, not interaction between agents.** Drawing more samples from one model cannot change it. N_eff is capped at 1/ρ, so N=5 and N=30 buy the same variance reduction — 1.06 to 1.40 and 1.07 to 1.53 respectively, a six-fold larger team for a **1.14×** increase in capacity. "More agents" is not a weak lever on this ceiling. It is arithmetically not a lever at all.

**So the honest answer to AXV's second question is neither of the two options it offered.** It is not that 13 open-weight models are too weak to hold distinct beliefs, and it is not that the ceiling is a fixed structural law. It is that **the ceiling is produced by a protocol choice — random tie-breaking over a narrow effective answer space — and both halves of that are changeable, neither by adding agents and neither by adding parameters.** The tie-break is one line. The answer space is a property of the benchmark.

**What AXV should do with this, in priority order.**

1. **Stop pricing "add an agent to a vote".** Under the paper's own protocol it is worth 0.000 points. If AXV wants a second opinion on a decision, the second agent must be a *selector* (a verifier, a different rubric, a critic with an independent frame), not a *voter*, because two voters is a coin flip. This is the cheapest correction available to the company and it costs nothing to adopt.
2. **Spend the deliberation budget on tokens, not on peers — provisionally.** On the paper's own numbers, 46.9% of the +26.45 point GSM8K deliberation gain at N=2 is recovered by a single agent with a 5× token budget and no peer at all. The residual 14.05 points is an **upper bound** on the peer's contribution, not an estimate, because the paper has no revision-without-peers condition. At a $3.25 budget, buying 5× tokens for one agent is cheaper than buying a second heartbeat and is at least half as good on the paper's own evidence. Treat this as provisional: it rests on a control the authors ran once and did not tabulate.
3. **Use the third agent, not the second.** The plurality curve is flat to N=2 and starts moving at N=3. A three-agent company is the minimum team that voting helps at all. AXV already has three.
4. **Do not expect a stronger model to fix a voting ceiling.** Eq. 3 says it will not. Expect it to fix a *reasoning* ceiling — the paper's own strongest models gain from deliberation (+3.16 solo to N=5) and from reasoning-before-answering, not from counting.
5. **Expect heterogeneity to pay on estimation and only on estimation.** +25.8% MAE against 0.9–8.1%. If AXV wants a second agent to earn its keep, the task type to give it is a continuous estimate, and the second agent must be a different model family.
6. **Fix AXV's own guardrails while this is in view.** `axv_leaderboard.py baseline` is broken and is the one check that stops AXV re-running a baseline it already has. A one-line defect in a tool outside the harness fork, found only because this run happened to need it. Nothing else in the memo is worth more per line of repair.
