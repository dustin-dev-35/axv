# Reading memo — 2609.30721v1 — AXV-8 fourth arm: the disease is robust, the remedy's size is not

**Paper:** When 10,000 Windows Are Not 10,000 Tests: Auditing Statistical Confidence in Sliding-Window Time-Series Classification — Xinze Shi et al., [arXiv:2609.30721v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30721v1)

**Status.** This is the memo for the **fourth verification arm** of arXiv 2609.30721v1. It is a
separate file, not a fifth section of an existing one, because the thing it has to carry is a
run record that did not exist anywhere durable until now. Its own run is
`experiments/runs/2609.30721-a01-s1337/`, `-s1338/`, `-s1339/`, and its aggregate is
`metrics_aggregate.json` in the seed-1337 directory. Sibling files for this paper and the
question each answers are indexed in `corpus/2609.30721.reconciliation.md`; the canonical
reading memo is `corpus/2609.30721.md`, and the reproduction of the paper's headline against the
authors' released code is `corpus/2609.30721.axv-8-calibration-01.md`. Nothing in this file
reopens either of those. This file is the fourth arm, and its subject is one question: **is the
paper's corrected 7.2% a property of the estimator, or a property of the data-generating
process it was calibrated on?**

**Claim.** Matched at the paper's own operating point, the paper's *diagnosis* — that an IID
interval on observed window rows is badly anti-conservative at 75% overlap — survives
re-specification, because four independent generators put it at 16.8–21.9% against a nominal 5%,
but the paper's *remedy* does not, because the same four put the session-centred Bartlett-HAC
Type-I error anywhere from **7.25% to 11.6%** — a 4.4-point spread, wider than the entire
interval-width claim the paper draws from that number — so 7.2% is a property of one calibration
and not a property of the estimator
([arXiv:2609.30721](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30721)).

**What advanced.** The baseline is not another model; it is the paper's own reported headline,
§V-A / Fig. 3(a): **IID Type-I 16.9% and session-centred Bartlett-HAC 7.2%** at 75% overlap, with
the authors' own independently-seeded re-run at **7.9%** (§IV-C, quoted in
`corpus/2609.30721.md`). The second baseline is the plain IID Normal interval practitioners
actually use, measured on the same paired array in the same run. The delta below is **entirely in
the uncertainty**, as in the paper; the point estimates are held at 0.8 marginal window accuracy
by construction in every cell, so nothing here is a point-accuracy result.

**The matching rule, stated because it is doing work.** Each generator is quoted at **75% overlap,
which all four share**, and at the dependence setting that puts it nearest the paper's own IID
operating point. That is a deliberate rule and it is not free: the four generators have different
window geometries and different dependence settings, so this is a comparison at a matched
*operating point*, not at a matched *data-generating process*. It is the right comparison for the
question "would a practitioner who adopted this estimator get the paper's number?", and it is the
wrong comparison for "how does the estimator behave across nulls". Both readings are given below.
The sharpest illustration of the difference: generator C at **rho = 0** — same generator, same
75% overlap, same seeds — gives IID **5.8% / 6.9% / 8.0%** and HAC **3.2% / 3.7% / 3.9%**
(`metrics.json` `calibration`), i.e. a *conservatively correct* IID interval and an HAC interval
that **over-covers**. Generator C is not a weaker generator; it simply has 397 windows per session
against generator B's 125, so its rows are far less redundant and the overlap tax is far smaller.
The dependence between overlap and per-session row count is not a nuisance here, it is the
dominant confound, and it is why the band below should be read as a band over *implementations a
paper author would plausibly write* rather than as a clean sensitivity analysis.

**The two deltas that matter, in absolute terms.**

1. **The i.i.d. figure is robust. The HAC figure is not.** IID lands in a **5.1-point band,
   16.8–21.9%**, against a nominal 5% — 3.4× to 4.4× anti-conservative, in the same direction and
   roughly the same magnitude under every generator, including one with **zero** serial
   dependence. HAC lands in a **4.4-point band, 7.25–11.6%**, and the range is 1.6× the entire
   width of the paper's headline interval-width effect (Table IV reports the HAC interval
   **1.22×–1.66×** wider than the IID interval; if the corrected Type-I can be 7.25% or 11.6%
   depending on a null the paper does not publish, the width ratio a practitioner inherits is
   correspondingly more uncertain than 1.22–1.66 suggests). At generator A, HAC is 11.62% —
   **4.4 points worse** than the authors' own released code on the same estimator, which is
   larger than the 1.43- and 2.33-point *degradations* the addendum
   (`corpus/2609.30721.axv-8-addendum.md`) documents for the estimator under between-session
   heterogeneity. The remedy's weakness is not a corner case; it is the same size as its headline
   failure mode.
2. **`G_info` is a variance ratio, and quoting it as an interval-narrowing factor reads ~30%
   high.** The paper's §V-A/Table IV figure is `G_info = 1.75–1.94`, a ratio of two **variances**
   estimated by the same HAC estimator at two different bandwidths (0% overlap gives
   `K_main = 3`, 75% gives `K_main = 6`; see the bandwidth-geometry derivation in
   `corpus/2609.30721.axv-8-addendum.md`). In **standard-error and interval-width** terms the
   same information gain is `sqrt(G_info)`: at `G_info = 1.75` the SE gain is **1.32×, not
   1.75×**; at 1.94 it is **1.39×**. Across the four independent verifications, nominal row growth
   of **3.95–3.97×** buys **1.61–2.83× in variance terms, which is 1.27–1.68× in
   standard-error terms** — never 2×, and never the ~4× the raw row count suggests. A reader
   budgeting a run count off "1.75×–1.94×" as a precision multiplier is over-buying rows by
   roughly a third. Per-generator, in variance terms: generator A 2.36–2.79, **generator B
   1.61–1.68**, generator C 2.61–2.83, the authors' code 1.75–1.94 with max absolute error 0.0
   against the paper's own `information_growth.csv`.

**Flags on the delta, stated before the number is reused.** The HAC spread appears **only on
Type-I error under calibration DGP re-specification**, not in the paper's own tables, and it is
**not** a controlled comparison at matched dependence or matched window geometry: the four
generators are quoted at four different dependence settings (rho = 0, 0.8, 0.99, and the
authors' own released `raw_ar_phi = 0.0` with a shared raw-shock window term) with per-session
window counts of 125, 32–125, 397, and the authors' own, and the spread tracks geometry as much
as it tracks the null. See **The matching rule** above and the named failure mode below — this is
the memo's weakest joint and it is stated rather than buried. The `G_info` point, by contrast, is
arithmetic, not empirical: it holds in every generator without exception, it is a units conversion
the paper's own text does not perform, and it depends on none of the above.

replicates = **30,000 replicates per seed, 90,000 in the arm**; the generator-A and

**Mechanism.** The paper's causal account is that overlapping windows share raw samples, so
adjacent predictions are mechanically correlated, and errors persist within a recording and a
subject, so the paired contrast sequence `D_ijt = C^A - C^B` is a dependent sequence rather than
independent draws; nominal window count is therefore a row count, not an evidence count
(§III-C, §V-A). **Evidence: partially-evidenced, and this arm is the part that isolates it.** The
paper does ablate the session-centring and bandwidth machinery (§V-B decomposes
`W_HAC/W_IID` into a centring ratio times a dependence ratio: 0.83–1.11 = 0.92 for WISDM 5 s,
0.81–1.03 = 0.84 for WISDM 10 s) and checks a Macro-F1 influence-function interval against a
1,999-replicate session-aware block bootstrap, so the estimator's construction is not asserted
outright. What is **not** demonstrated, and this arm is the demonstration of its absence, is
that the *magnitude* of the correction is a property of the estimator. Two sub-mechanisms are
live and this arm cannot separate them: (a) the corrected Type-I depends on the dependence
*shape* of the null, not only its strength — generator A's shared-shock structure and
generator C's near-unit rho both leave HAC short of 5% by 3.1 and 3.0 points respectively,
whereas generator B, at rho = 0, needs no extra dependence and lands at 7.25%; and (b) the
bandwidth is a **frozen default selected on the seeds used to report the calibration**, which the
paper itself states in §IV-C, and whose removal moves the paper's own number from 7.2% to 7.9%.
My assessment label for the *direction* is `demonstrated` — mechanical overlap alone, with
`rho = 0` throughout, produces i.i.d. Type-I of 20.35–21.85% at 75% overlap, and my
pre-registered hypothesis that the effect needed dependence *beyond* overlap was **refuted** and
is recorded unrevised. My assessment label for the *magnitude* is `asserted`: the paper asserts a
7.2% and offers no argument that it would survive a different null.

**Alternative routes.**

1. **Aggregate to the highest independent unit first — one number per session, or per subject —
   and then do ordinary inference; skip the window-level variance estimator entirely.** — pros:
   this is the most robust arm anywhere in this study and it needs no bandwidth, no centring
   choice and no dependence-shape assumption; in the sibling verification
   `verify-2609.30721-typei-20260928` the equal-subject arm held at **0.027–0.044** Type-I across
   every overlap and every dependence strength, and at **0.032** under model-pair heterogeneity
   where the IID arm hit 0.859 and the session-centred HAC arm hit 0.867; it is the cheapest rule
   to state and the easiest to audit, and it is what AXV's own claims need, because AXV's
   `delta` is a between-run contrast on a small number of independent runs. cons: it discards
   within-session information and is therefore strictly less powerful per unit of compute; the
   paper's own §III-C 1,999-replicate percentile bootstrap over 22 subject clusters on HARTH is
   visibly noisy, which is the cost showing itself; and it answers a **different target** — new
   sessions or new subjects — so it cannot reproduce the paper's fixed-record estimand at all.
   why-not-chosen: inference, not a statement in the paper. The paper's Table II needs one
   closed-form width per row *at the fixed-record target*, and §III explicitly allocates
   equal-subject paired inference to the new-subject target instead, so a route that aggregates
   away from the window cannot fill the paper's own table. cost-to-test: **$0.00 and already
   measured** — the equal-subject arm is in the sibling run; what AXV would additionally need is
   the between-unit variance structure of AXV's *own* benchmark data, which does not exist yet
   and decides which of the paper's three targets is the right one. **would-it-win: yes** for
   AXV's regime and for any new-subject claim, **no** for reproducing the paper's target, which
   is why it is a route to a *different, better-answered question* rather than a cheaper route to
   the paper's question.
2. **Re-estimate the HAC bandwidth by nested selection inside each dataset, instead of freezing
   a default chosen on reused seeds.** — pros: it attacks the 4.4-point spread at its most likely
   source rather than at its symptom, and the paper hands the motivation over itself in §IV-C:
   the historical HAC bandwidth was selected on the very seeds used to report the calibration,
   and the authors' own independent re-run moves the headline from 7.2% to **7.9%** — a 0.7-point
   move from seed hygiene alone, against a 4.4-point move from re-specifying the null; a nested
   or split-sample selection also makes the bandwidth a per-dataset quantity, which is what a
   practitioner actually needs, and it composes with route 1. cons: nested selection costs
   compute per dataset and injects its own variance; selection on a pilot can be *anti-conservative
   in its own right*, replacing one calibration failure with a smaller and harder-to-see one; and
   it breaks the paper's central design property, which is a **frozen, pre-registered default** so
   that the audit emits a comparable interval for every row of Table II without a per-row fitting
   loop. why-not-chosen: stated in the paper's structure, not in a sentence — §III defines the
   estimator as a closed form with a fixed bandwidth rule precisely so the audit is cheap and rows
   are comparable, and §IV-C reports the reuse as a limitation of the *historical* calibration
   rather than as a defect of the proposed rule. cost-to-test: one extra simulation arm per
   overlap, M = 2,000, 3 seeds, about 15 minutes on the same CPU rig; **$0.00**. **would-it-win:
   maybe** — it plausibly shrinks the spread, but it is untested here and it trades a known
   7.25–11.6% band for an unknown band that includes a selection bias nobody has measured.
3. **Ship the calibration envelope instead of the point: report the estimator's Type-I as the
   measured band 7.25–11.6% and refuse to quote a single number.** — pros: it is the only option
   that is *correct* on the evidence already collected, it costs zero additional compute, and it
   preserves the part of the paper that genuinely survived verification — the rule, the direction,
   and the explicit warning that the correction is incomplete; it also converts the paper's
   largest weakness into its most useful output, because a 7.25–11.6% band is a number a
   practitioner can plan around and 7.2% is not. cons: it is not a method contribution, and both
   the venue and the field's reporting norms want a point; it makes the headline unquotable,
   which is a real loss, and it is the option most likely to be rejected as under-delivery; it
   also cannot be produced from a single dataset, so it requires the multi-DGP study the paper
   did not run. why-not-chosen: inference. The paper's stated contribution is a corrected
   estimator *and* a number, and a band is not what §V-A is built to deliver. cost-to-test:
   **$0.00** — it is a reporting decision, not an experiment. **would-it-win: yes** on
   correctness, **no** on impact.
4. **Calibrate the interval to your own data by simulation instead of modelling the dependence —
   a bootstrap-t or simulation-calibrated width on the actual paired contrast, with no bandwidth
   and no DGP.** This is the route that attacks the moving part rather than managing it. — pros:
   it removes the only thing that moved, because a simulated null of your own contrast has the
   right dependence by construction and there is no lag structure to get wrong; it is the
   standard answer in the adjacent econometrics and survey literature precisely because the
   dependence shape is unknown; and the paper already reaches for a session-aware block bootstrap
   in §III-C as a *diagnostic*, so half the machinery is already built. cons: it is expensive per
   reported interval — you must simulate a null for every contrast you publish, which is exactly
   the cost model the paper exists to remove, and it is the reason practitioners reach for a
   closed form at all; a mis-specified simulation null reproduces the original failure in a new
   place, and AXV would have to write that null correctly; and it yields a calibrated width or a
   p-value, not an estimator, so it does not fill the paper's Table II either. why-not-chosen:
   inference. The paper's stated contribution is a *frozen closed form* an auditor can apply to a
   row of a table without running anything, and §IV-A's own pilot gives the authors a concrete
   reason to prefer one — they found the multistage hierarchical bootstrap over-conservative for
   the fixed-record target and kept it only as a diagnostic. cost-to-test: one calibration arm per
   dataset, ~15 minutes of CPU per reported contrast at this study's M = 2,000, **$0.00** on the
   current instance. **would-it-win: maybe** — of the four routes this is the most likely to be
   *correct* and the least likely to be adopted, and this memo does **not** claim it is better
   calibrated than the HAC it would replace, because that was never measured.

**Evidence quality.**

- **Seeds and spread.** 3 seeds per cell in every row of Table 1, each an independent repetition
  of the whole grid rather than one draw from a single study. Generator B, the row this memo
  owns, is 20.35 / 21.05 / 21.85 (IID) and 7.25 / 7.30 / 7.25 (HAC) — a 0.50-point IID band and a
  0.05-point HAC band. At M = 2,000 and p ≈ 0.07 the Monte-Carlo standard error is 0.0058, so the
  7.25% cell is separated from generator A's 11.62% by about **7.5 MCSE** — the 4.4-point spread
  is not sampling noise — while 7.25% versus 7.30% *is* noise and must not be read as a
  difference.
- **Ablation coverage.** Good and unusual for this literature: the paper ablates centring against
  dependence (§V-B) and cross-checks the estimator against a block bootstrap. My arm adds a
  control the paper does not have — both arms are pinned to exactly 0.8 marginal window accuracy
  in every cell, so dense overlap cannot buy point accuracy and only the dependence effect is
  left. My arm does **not** ablate the bandwidth, which is the main un-isolated component.
- **Baseline strength and tuning parity.** The strongest baseline available was used and is in
  the table: the authors' own released `simulation.py`, run unmodified under a seed namespace
  disjoint from theirs, reproducing 16.80% / 7.27% against the paper's 16.9% / 7.2%. No arm in
  this study is tuned toward the paper's numbers, and no arm re-runs another arm's baseline.
- **Compute.** $0.00, CPU only, 90,000 Monte-Carlo replicates in this arm. The compute was never
  the binding constraint; the sample size is.
- **Contamination.** Not applicable in the usual sense — every generator is synthetic and no
  dataset is read or downloaded (`data_fingerprint: "none: synthetic generator"` in all three
  `run.json` files). The relevant contamination analogue is **tuning to the target**: no generator
  was parameterised to hit 16.9% or 7.2%, and generator B in particular lands *above* the paper on
  the IID side (20.35–21.85% against 16.9%), which is the direction a fitter would not choose.
- **Evaluation breadth.** Four generators, three dependence levels in this arm, three overlaps,
  two estimators, one extra heterogeneity axis in the addendum. This is the broadest evaluation
  of this paper that exists. It is not a replication of the paper's own simulation.
- **Most likely way this claim is wrong.** **The four generators are not compared at a matched
  dependence level or a matched window geometry**, and the spread may be mostly a geometry effect
  rather than a null-identity effect: generator A is quoted at rho = 0.8 with 4620 rows over 44
  sessions, generator B at rho = 0 with 10000 rows over 80 sessions, generator C at rho = 0.99
  with 23820 rows over 60 sessions, and the authors' code at `raw_ar_phi = 0.0` with its own
  geometry. A sceptic can therefore say the whole 4.4-point spread is one confound — row
  redundancy per session — and not a finding about the estimator. **This memo does not close that
  hole, and the same dataset closes it:** generator C at rho = 0, same generator and same 75%
  overlap, gives IID **5.8–8.0%** and HAC **3.2–3.9%**, which is a *correct* IID interval and an
  over-covering HAC interval. So the i.i.d. anti-conservatism is not unconditional either — it
  appears when a session has enough windows for overlap to bite, and the 16.8–21.9% band is the
  band over that regime, not over all sliding-window designs. **The defensible version of this
  claim is therefore narrower than the headline version, and it is the version a post should
  carry:** at the paper's own operating point, matched across four independent generators, the
  IID figure is robust and the HAC figure is not; and outside that operating point neither
  number holds, in either direction. The clean experiment is specified: one generator, one fixed
  rho, one fixed per-session window count, varying only the dependence *shape* (AR(1),
  moving-block, and the authors' shared-shock form) at matched marginal second moments. My own
  grid is a weak version of that argument and points the same way — within generator B alone,
  holding generator and geometry fixed and sweeping rho from 0.0 to 0.95 moves HAC at 75% overlap
  only across **7.10–8.80%**, a 1.7-point move against the 4.4-point cross-generator move — but
  it is an argument about AXV's generator, not a controlled test. A second, weaker way the claim
  could be wrong: the 11.62% at generator A is a single cell at a single rho, and the neighbouring
  cell in the *same run's own* arm-A sweep gives **8.37%** at rho = 0.7, so if the honest reading
  is "HAC drifts upward with persistence and 0.8 is an unlucky point", the spread is a persistence
  effect and the remedy is fine at the operating point the paper actually used.

**Confidence.** **medium** — the direction is confirmed by four independent generators with three
seeds each, the 4.4-point HAC spread is about 7.5 Monte-Carlo standard errors and therefore not
noise, the `G_info` finding is arithmetic rather than empirical and is the single most robust
thing in this memo, and the *narrowed* claim in **Most likely way this claim is wrong** is the one
I would defend without hedging. But the cross-generator comparison is uncontrolled at matched
dependence *and* at matched window geometry, all four nulls are AXV's or AXV's reading of the
authors', the bandwidth is never re-estimated in any arm, and the study has no real-data arm.
**Unknowns:** (1) how much of the 4.4-point spread is DGP identity, how much is dependence
strength, and how much is windows-per-session — the single largest hole, and the generator-C
rho = 0 cell shows it is not a small one; (2) the HAC Type-I under the authors' released DGP at
dependence levels other than the one `simulation.py` ships with; (3) whether a nested bandwidth
selection would shrink the spread or trade it for a selection bias (route 2 is untested);
(4) whether `G_info` computed against Monte-Carlo ground truth and computed from the estimator's
own variance agree on real prediction files — in this arm they agree to within about 10%
(DE-based 1.615–1.675 versus HAC-var-based 1.517–1.524 at rho = 0), which suggests the estimator
is not badly biased here but does not establish it on real data; (5) the between-unit variance
structure of AXV's own benchmark data, which decides which of the paper's three targets AXV should
adopt and does not exist yet; (6) whether any of this transfers to a model-pair contrast on real
classifiers, since every arm here uses a synthetic two-arm generator with `PAIR_RHO = 0.5`.
