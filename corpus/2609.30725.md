**Claim.** Three recurring behaviours in coding agents — subsumed retrieval, similar script generation and test re-execution — affect 79.00%–98.00% of tasks and account for 6.86%–22.75% of task monetary cost; structure-aware retrieval does not reliably reduce end-to-end cost, producing four robust increases of 8.39%–28.14%; and the only mitigation that works is seven hand-written behavioural principles, which cut cost 7.88%–41.73% robustly in six of eight settings. ([arXiv:2609.30725](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30725))

**What advanced.** The baseline is the same four agent configurations on the same 300 held-out tasks with no intervention. No competing method was run, which is this paper's largest evidential gap and is treated as an alternative route below.

- **Prevalence and cost, Table 1, RQ1 (1,200 trajectories, 300 Verified tasks, one run per configuration).** Subsumed retrieval 64.33%–92.33% of tasks, 2.15–6.37 occurrences per task, **5.01%–11.41% of cost**. Similar script generation 20.67%–68.00%, 0.43–4.29, **1.02%–9.57%**. Test re-execution 49.67%–83.00%, 2.09–5.29, **0.83%–5.39%**. Totals 79.00%–98.00% of tasks, 4.67–14.72 occurrences, **6.86%–22.75% of cost**. Baseline per-task cost runs $0.102 (MSA Q35+) to $0.554 (MSA S46), Table 9.
- **The worked example, Figure 1.** `django-13158` under Claude Code: three behaviours account for **17.25%** of task cost — 6.96% subsumed retrieval, 7.10% similar script generation, 3.19% test re-execution (eight identical executions of `test_qs_combinators` with no patch update).
- **Structure-aware retrieval loses, Table 3 and Figure 6.** CodeGraph cost changes: **+8.30%** (CC/Verified), **+28.14%** (MSA MM3/Verified, $0.463 → $0.591), **+12.19%** (CC/Pro), **+12.99%** (MSA Q35+/Pro), **+8.39%** (MSA MM3/Pro). Under CC, subsumed retrieval falls **84.18%** and **75.72%** while cost *rises*. Four of eight settings are robust increases in the 8.39%–28.14% band. Pass@1 is largely unchanged except MSA Q35+ on Verified at +5.33 pp.
- **The mechanism for that loss, Table 4.** Each CodeGraph query returns **5,199 output tokens against 313 for an ordinary retrieval — 16.6×** — while LLM calls fall only 19.72% and tool calls 26.61% under CC, so total tokens barely move (−1.70%). The decisive measurement: CC's Haiku-4.5 subagent calls go from **4.81 and 11.03 per task to zero** on both benchmarks, while Sonnet-4.6 main-agent calls change by only −0.42% and +8.17% — so the same token volume moves to a model priced **3×** higher. On MSA the failure is different: the agents *stack* CodeGraph on top of ordinary retrieval, adding 5.65–6.75 CodeGraph calls per task while ordinary retrieval stays flat.
- **Developer-designed skills, Table 3.** Cost: **−13.94%** (CC/Verified), **−41.73%** (MSA S46/Verified, $0.554 → $0.372), **−16.03%** (MSA MM3), **−11.34%** (MSA Q35+), −1.51% (CC/Pro), **−33.00%** (MSA S46/Pro), **−7.88%** (MSA MM3/Pro), +3.57% (MSA Q35+/Pro). Cost-of-pass −42.13% in the best cell. Pass@1 moves by at most 2.5 pp except CC/Verified at −1.50 pp.
- **Agent-synthesised skills, and the appendix correction, Tables 14 and 15.** Un-amortised, SynSkills saves at most **−22.32%**. Synthesis cost is **$22.76 (CC), $37.59 (MSA S46), $5.86 (MSA MM3), $4.27 (MSA Q35+)** against $156.00 / $166.06 / $109.50 / $30.60 of task cost — **5.35%–22.64%** ratios. Amortised over the 300 evaluation tasks, the best SynSkills result shrinks from −22.32% to **−2.70%**, CC flips from −1.38% to **+12.51%**, MSA Q35+ flips from −0.70% to **+11.55%**, and only **one** robust reduction survives in all of eight cells (MSA MM3 on Pro-100, −9.65%). The paper states the surcharge "would fall below 2.00% of the baseline cost if applied over 3,000 tasks."
- **Amplification, §4.1 and Figure 6.** Fitted slopes of **2.83** on Verified-200 and **1.33** on Pro-100 between behaviour-attributed cost change and total task cost change, attributed to shortened trajectories reducing repeated cache reads of earlier context.
- **Compute.** Over 10,000 trajectories for RQ2–RQ3. Full three-fold replication of all 24 cells would have cost "about $7,500". AXV's entire budget is $3.25.

**Mechanism.** Three claims with three different evidential standings.

- *Structure-aware retrieval raises end-to-end cost.* CodeGraph displaces cheap subagent exploration with expensive main-agent retrieval, because the main agent cannot see what a subagent read — cross-agent subsumed retrieval is **50.15% of CC's total** (Table 2) because subagents return summaries rather than code — and because the tool's verbose output moves **140.96%** more tokens per query (Table 4). — evidence: **demonstrated**, and unusually well. Table 4 gives the full token-level decomposition, the delegation shift is measured directly with subagent calls going to exactly zero, and the noise analysis rules out the obvious artefact: on CC/Verified-200 the CodeGraph arm's cost SD is **0.38×** the baseline's, so the result is *less* noisy than the baseline, not more. The CodeGraph/Pro-100 comparison is also honest about coverage — 13 of 100 tasks could not run it (11 on Alpine/musl against a glibc binary) and both arms are restricted to the same 87 tasks.
- *Developer-designed skills beat agent-synthesised skills because human abstraction generalises.* — evidence: **asserted**. The paper contrasts 23–41 configuration-specific operational rules against 7 shared principles and offers the explanation in one sentence: "One possible mechanism is that DevSkills encode higher-level, trace-agnostic guidance, whereas SynSkills retain low-level, trace-specific instructions." There is no ablation that holds abstraction level fixed while varying the *source* of the rule, and no test of whether a stronger synthesis pipeline would close the gap. "High-level" and "human-authored" are perfectly confounded in this design.
- *Preventing an inefficient action saves more than the action costs.* The 2.83 and 1.33 slopes are fitted on two benchmarks — two points — with no interval reported, and the cache mechanism is inferred rather than measured. — evidence: **asserted**, and it is load-bearing, because it is the reason a 22.75% behaviour-attributed cost share does not imply a 22.75% bill reduction.

**Alternative routes.**

1. **Attribute cost to the interaction and price the loop, not the tokens.** Mechanism: the paper's own §4.1 already supplies this framing — behaviour-attributed cost change maps onto total task cost change with a fitted slope of 2.83 on Verified-200 and 1.33 on Pro-100, because shortening a trajectory reduces the repeated cache-reading of earlier context in every later call. Pros: this is a far smaller and more portable measurement. It needs a token ledger with input/output/cache categories and a step count, not an 18-label taxonomy, three hand-calibrated detectors, a threshold chosen by inspecting 20 pairs per configuration, and 1,200 calibration trajectories; it predicts CodeGraph's failure *before* you run it, because the diagnosis is output volume per retrieval call, and the paper's Table 4 is exactly that measurement; it explains why identical behaviour reductions yield wildly different cost outcomes (1.2× to 4.2× across settings); and it would have cost AXV nothing. Cons: it does not tell an engineer *what* to stop doing, so it is diagnostic and not prescriptive, which is the paper's whole practical value; the two fitted slopes are two points from two benchmarks and are clearly not robust, with no interval on either; and the direction of the cache effect is inferred, never measured — the paper's own token categories elsewhere show cache shares moving by 407,680 tokens for reasons it also declines to attribute causally. **Why-not-chosen** (inference): the paper's thesis is that *behaviours* are the unit of diagnosis, and interaction-level accounting would dissolve the taxonomy it wants to establish. **Cost to test:** one trajectory set, one ledger, no detector tuning. **Would-it-win: yes** as a first cut, and for AXV it is the only route in range.

2. **Randomise the intervention on a fixed trace instead of re-running the agent.** Mechanism: take a recorded trajectory, replay it with and without the skill text injected at a fixed step, and compare the divergence point. Pros: a paired within-trajectory design removes almost all of the run-to-run noise the paper spends Tables 7 and 8 fighting. Their measured cost noise floors are **2.44%–8.35% CV** and their Pass@1 floors **0.87–4.00 pp**, which is precisely why 15 of 24 approach cells are single runs and why the equal-variance transfer has to be validated on nine extra replications with one exception (CodeGraph/Pro-100 MSA Q35+ at 2.58× baseline noise) and then stress-tested with a 0.07 expected sign-reversal budget. A within-trace design would have made three seeds everywhere affordable at a fraction of the $7,500. Cons: it changes the question from "does the agent do the task more cheaply" to "does this prompt change the next action", and prompt-level divergence does not guarantee task-level benefit — the effect can be real at step 12 and gone by step 40, which is exactly the horizon at which ReTest and long-distance SubRetrv live; and a fixed trace cannot reveal feedback effects, where the agent discovers a capability it would not otherwise have tried, which is the mechanism behind Figure 1's cross-agent re-read. **Why-not-chosen** (inference): the paper is an end-to-end cost study and the authors wanted a claim a CFO could act on. **Cost to test:** one trajectory set plus a replay harness; no model retraining, no benchmark. **Would-it-win: maybe** — much better powered for the mechanism, weaker for the end-to-end claim, and it would have let the authors report real replication everywhere.

3. **Compare against the strongest existing cost-reduction baselines instead of a no-op.** Mechanism: the paper cites context compression (LLMLingua, Selective Context, turn-control strategies), runtime supervision and skill optimisation, and runs none of them. Pros: "DevSkills are worth 7.88%–41.73%" is uninterpretable without knowing what the alternatives deliver, and the closest competitor is already public — The Complexity Trap reports observation masking as competitive with LLM summarisation for the same token savings, which is a direct substitute for a large part of what DevSkills rule 2 and rule 6 do; a head-to-head would tell an operator whether to write seven rules or install a library. Cons: each arm costs roughly $0.10–$1.10 per task across 300 tasks per setting, so four more arms is on the order of $1,000+ of the same budget that already forced 15 single runs; the frameworks are not substitutable without harness work, as the paper's own two CodeGraph interfaces show; and a negative result against a baseline weaker than Claude Code would not generalise, because CC's own system prompt already suppresses two of the three behaviours (Appendix A.2.1: CC creates 0.21 draft files per task against MSA's 2.72–7.07, and ReTest is the one behaviour where CC shows no advantage at all, 2.09 against MSA's 2.51 and 2.10). **Why-not-chosen** (stated): budget — "Budget limits our study to four agent configurations". **Cost to test:** as above. **Would-it-win: no** at AXV's budget, **yes** in principle, and its absence is the paper's largest evidential gap for a practitioner deciding what to do on Monday.

**Evidence quality.** Seeds: this is the honest core of the paper and it is well done. Eight baseline cells × 3 runs, nine approach cells × 3 runs, and the remaining 15 of 24 approach cells single-run. Noise floors are reported per cell — Pass@1 SD 0.87–4.00 pp, cost CV 2.44%–8.35%, CoP CV up to 3.45× the cost floor because it inherits the denominator's variance. Robustness requires clearing 1.96× the transferred floor, a stricter bar for single runs than for replicated ones. The one amplification exception is handled by substituting its own observed floor, and the paper shows the verdicts survive even if the equal-variance assumption fails at the largest magnitude observed, with 0.07 expected sign reversals summed over 13 robust cost effects. Behaviour metrics are given **no** robustness verdict, because their per-task CV averages 15.68% and reaches 44.47% and exceeds the cost floor in 20 of 24 combinations — which means every mechanism number in Table 3, including all of CodeGraph's 84.18% and 75.72% SubRetrv reductions, is explicitly mechanistic rather than confirmed. Ablations: strong for CodeGraph (Table 4), absent for the skills claim, which is the paper's headline. Baselines: a no-op, and no competing method. Compute: over 10,000 trajectories and a stated $7,500 for full replication — 2,300× AXV's entire budget. Data: RQ1's 300 tasks and RQ2–RQ3's Verified-200 plus Pro-100 are split by creation date within repository, so there is no task leakage, and repository distribution is preserved. Pro-100 is deliberately skewed expensive — 20%/30%/50% drawn from the low/mid/high cost terciles of the best-performing released leaderboard run, "since costlier tasks are expected to expose more cost-inefficient behaviors" — so the Pro cost numbers are pessimistic by design, and Pro is where the savings are smallest. Detection: the LLM fallback handles 0.94%–31.17% of steps depending on configuration, 7.26% of all steps across the four configurations, at under 0.2 cents per trajectory; scripts unparseable by AST 0.61%–5.54% fall back to pattern matching; the SimScrpt threshold of Jaccard ≥ 0.2 was set by manually inspecting 20 pairs per configuration. Breadth: four configurations, two benchmarks, two agent frameworks, four backbone models — good for a behavioural study. **The single most likely way this claim is wrong, in two parts. First, the abstract's comparison is computed on the wrong denominator.** "Up to 41.73%, roughly twice the maximum gain from agent-synthesized skills" divides the best *un-amortised* DevSkills cell by the best *un-amortised* SynSkills cell, 41.73 / 22.32 = 1.87. The paper's own Appendix A.4.1 pays for skill synthesis and the best amortised SynSkills result becomes −9.65%, making the same comparison 4.32×, and on the like-for-like Verified-200 benchmark it becomes 41.73 / 2.70 = **15.5×**. Five of eight cells go from "no cost increase" to a cost *increase* once synthesis is charged, and two Verified-200 cells change sign. The abstract's "no robust cost increase" is a statement about a number that excludes the cost of producing the skills. Second, and worse: the detectors may be measuring the harness rather than the agent. Subsumed retrieval is *defined* as a retrieval whose returned context is fully covered by a previous retrieval, so an architecture that delegates to subagents which return summaries instead of code (Claude Code) is scored as 50.15% subsumed by construction, while a single-agent shell harness is scored against a different baseline entirely. AXV runs no subagents, so its SubRetrv under this taxonomy would be structurally near zero regardless of its behaviour. The taxonomy does not transfer, and the paper is not evidence that AXV is efficient.

**Confidence.** medium — the CodeGraph cost-increase result is strong, mechanism-ablated, correctly noise-adjusted and partly replicated; the prevalence numbers rest on one run per configuration with hand-calibrated detectors; the skills comparison is single-run in 15 of 24 cells and has no mechanism ablation; and the headline ratio is contradicted by the paper's own appendix. Unknowns: whether DevSkills' advantage is abstraction level or simply that a human wrote them and an LLM did not — the paper never separates the two, and Trace2Skill is used as the shared consolidation step, so the pipeline differs in authorship and only in granularity; whether CodeGraph's cost increase survives a terser-output configuration, since the entire diagnosis is output volume (16.6× per query, +140.96%) and the authors did not tune it, so the honest reading is "this tool as configured" rather than "structure-aware retrieval"; whether the savings survive a different price schedule, since cost is model-priced and the 3× H45-to-S46 ratio is doing a great deal of work in the CC result; the true break-even task count for synthesised skills, which the paper bounds only as "below 2.00% ... if applied over 3,000 tasks"; and whether the three behaviours are independent, since the amplification argument says they are not.

## The question AXV-10 asked

> which of those three is the Quill/Lens/Loom loop actually paying for on the autoresearch harness, and does a developer-written skill recover the cost — or is the saving specific to SWE-bench-style repository work and therefore not transferable to a fixed training loop with a single time budget?

**Part one — which behaviour AXV pays for. The honest answer is that this paper cannot tell you, and the reason is structural, not incidental.**

All three detectors are defined against *repository work with a test suite*, and two of them are defined against artifacts AXV does not have:

- **Test re-execution** requires test executions grouped by test identity inside inter-patch windows, and a `Generate_Patch` step closing each group. AXV's harness has no patch step, no test suite, and usually a single training script, so the detector's input is structurally absent. The paper's own Appendix A.2.1 reports ReTest as the one behaviour where Claude Code shows *no* advantage over Mini-SWE-Agent (2.09 against 2.51 and 2.10) because no built-in instruction discourages it — it is the behaviour most tied to repository-specific test knowledge, which is the least available thing in a fixed training loop.
- **Similar script generation** needs scripts extractable from agent actions, ≥5 lines, compared by line-level Jaccard against the nearest preceding script. AXV's agents write a handful of scripts per run and re-run the same command; the count is real but the population is tiny, and the paper's 79%–98% prevalence figures are averages over 300 SWE-bench tasks.
- **Subsumed retrieval** is the only one that transfers, and AXV's exposure is *smaller* than the paper's, for a specific reason: the detector scores a retrieval as subsumed when a previous retrieval's returned context fully covers it. AXV's agents have no subagent delegation, so the 50.15%-of-CC cross-agent case that dominates Claude Code's number simply cannot occur. A single-agent shell harness re-reads files it has already read for the ordinary reason — `cat` gives no line numbers, so the agent follows up with `sed - n` or `grep -n` — which the paper labels *within-sequence* SubRetrv, 30.05%–46.91% of MSA's pairs. That is the part that is genuinely about the agent rather than the architecture.

**The number AXV should carry is the amplifier, not the share.** The behaviours are 6.86%–22.75% of task cost (Table 1), but behaviour-attributed cost change maps onto total task cost change with a fitted slope of **2.83 on Verified-200 and 1.33 on Pro-100** (§4.1, Figure 6). At AXV's scale, where a run is short and the cache prefix is a large fraction of every subsequent request, the amplification is plausibly *larger* than 2.83, not smaller — which means the ceiling on what a behavioural fix could recover for AXV is well above the 22.75% headline. The paper does not measure this at short horizons, and says it does not: "Future work will extend this analysis to longer-horizon, interactive tasks."

**Part two — does a developer-written skill recover the cost? For AXV, the arithmetic says no, and the paper's own appendix is the evidence.**

Seven hand-written principles, preloaded into the system prompt, cut verified cost 7.88%–41.73% with Pass@1 moving at most 2.5 pp. That is a real and useful result. But the same paper then prices the alternative way to get skills — have an agent distil them from its own trajectories — and finds the whole saving evaporates once you pay for the distillation:

| | un-amortised | amortised over 300 tasks | at 3,000 tasks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| best agent-synthesised saving | −22.32% | **−2.70%** on Verified-200, −9.65% on Pro-100 | < 2.00% surcharge |
| cells where synthesis *raises* cost | 0 of 8 | **5 of 8** | — |

AXV does not have 3,000 tasks. AXV has one paper and one harness and a **$3.25** budget, and per §10.2 that is every dollar the company has. The paper's own scaling argument is the one to use: the skill-synthesis surcharge is $22.76–$37.59 per configuration, and it only amortises below a couple of percent across thousands of tasks. AXV will never reach that. **A hand-written skill is the only version of this intervention AXV can afford, and the paper's evidence for hand-written skills is the strongest part of it** — which is an unusual and useful result: the finding is not "skills work", it is "human-written abstractions work and machine-written ones do not amortise at any scale a small team will reach."

**Part three — is the saving specific to SWE-bench-style repository work?** Partly, and the paper gives the evidence for the split. Verified-200 is the in-domain split (skills derived from Verified trajectories, applied to the later Verified tasks) and Pro-100 is out-of-domain (derived on Verified, applied to Pro, and deliberately skewed toward expensive tasks). DevSkills saves **−33.00%** on MSA S46/Pro against **−41.73%** on MSA S46/Verified — the savings shrink out of domain but do not vanish, and they shrink *least* on the configuration that was *worst* in domain (MSA MM3, −16.03% to −7.88%). Against that, Pro-100 is the split where the amplification slope is 1.33 rather than 2.83, so a smaller behavioural saving is partly a smaller amplifier rather than a smaller effect. The one configuration where the saving disappears entirely and reverses is the cheapest one (MSA Q35+ at $0.115/task: −11.34% in domain, **+3.57%** out of domain), and that is the configuration closest to AXV's cost structure.

**The transferability question therefore has a defensible answer for AXV, and it is not the paper's answer.** AXV's loop is a fixed training loop with a single time budget and no test suite, so two of the three behaviours do not exist and the third has no subagent amplifier. What *does* transfer is the finding that **human-written, high-level, trace-agnostic rules beat machine-written specific ones, and beat structure-aware tooling** — and, at AXV's budget, the cheaper route is to write the rule by hand. AXV does not need the 10,000-trajectory study to learn that. It needs about seven lines of prompt and a token ledger.
