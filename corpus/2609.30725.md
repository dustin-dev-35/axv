# Reading memo — 2609.30725v1 — Analyzing and Mitigating Cost-Inefficient Behaviors in Coding Agents

**Paper:** Analyzing and Mitigating Cost-Inefficient Behaviors in Coding Agents - Yiran Hu et al., [arXiv:2609.30725](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30725v1)

**Claim.** Coding agents waste a measurable share of their budget on three recurring
behaviours, and a small set of high-level, trace-agnostic *developer-written* skills recover
roughly twice as much cost as agent-synthesised skills do
([arXiv:2609.30725](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30725)).

**What advanced.** The baselines are three, not one: always-on (no mitigation),
structure-aware retrieval (CodeGraph), and agent-synthesised skills. The delta is on Table 3
(2 benchmarks × 4 agent configurations × 3 approaches) and Table 4. Developer-designed skills
**robustly reduce average task cost in six of eight settings by 7.88%–41.73%**, with the
maximum at **−41.73%** on Mini-SWE-Agent with Sonnet 4.6 on Verified-200 and the minimum
robust at **−7.88%** on MiniMax-M3 on Pro-100. Absolute dollars: MSA/S46 Verified-200
$0.605–0.674 baseline → **$0.372**; CC Verified-200 $0.515–0.598 → **$0.473**; CC Pro-100
$0.881–0.922 → **$0.894** (+1.51%, not robust). Agent-synthesised skills robustly reduce cost
in only **three of eight** settings, by **8.86%–22.32%** — and on appendix amortisation, once
the $37.59 and $22.76 synthesis costs are charged, the two large savings collapse to
**−2.70% and −0.49%**, MSAQ35+ flips to *robust increases* of +11.55% and +8.48%, and **no
setting keeps a robust CoP improvement**. Structure-aware retrieval is the negative result and
the paper is candid about it: **no robust cost reduction anywhere, and four robust cost
increases of 8.39%–28.14%**, despite cutting Claude Code's SubRetrv by 75.72–84.18% on
Verified-200. The mechanism is quantified in Table 4: CodeGraph cuts LLM calls 19.72–23.21%
and tool calls 26.61–31.47%, but each CodeGraph query returns **8.2x–16.6x** more tokens than
an ordinary retrieval, and in Claude Code it drives Haiku-4.5 subagent calls from 4.81 and
11.03 per task to **zero** while main Sonnet-4.6 calls barely move (−0.42%, +8.17%) — so the
same token volume shifts to a model priced **3x** higher, which is the entire
+8.30%/+12.19% regression. The prevalence result is Table 1: the three behaviours hit
**79.00%–98.00%** of tasks and account for **6.86%–22.75%** of task cost, with SubRetrv the
largest (64.33%–92.33% of tasks, 5.01%–11.41% of cost). Compute: **1,200 trajectories** for
RQ1 (4 configurations × 300 SWE-bench Verified tasks, each configuration run **once**) and
**over 10k trajectories** for RQ2–RQ3 on Verified-200 plus Pro-100. Eval protocol: Pass@1, task
monetary cost, cost-of-pass, and behaviour counts; robust at one-sided p < 0.05 against a
**transferred baseline noise floor**. Flags, and they are serious: **15 of 24 approach cells
are single runs.** The full design (2 × 4 × 4 × 3 = 96 runs) would have cost about **$7,500**;
the authors ran 8 baselines × 3 plus 9 approach cells × 3 and transferred the rest. No token
price schedule is published anywhere — §A.1.2 says only "the corresponding model's token
prices" — so every dollar figure in the paper is unreproducible, and the only price datum given
is the 3x S46:H45 ratio. Pro-100 was deliberately **biased toward costlier tasks**
(20%/30%/50% across low/mid/high terciles of a leaderboard cost ranking dated June 26, 2026).
CodeGraph could not run on 13 of the 100 Pro-100 tasks (11 Alpine/musl vs glibc, 2
unexplained), so its Pro numbers are over 87 tasks. On amortisation, the paper's own analysis
says the SynSkills advantage "depends on the deployment scale rather than on the skills
themselves".

**Mechanism.** The causal account is that the three behaviours are architectural, not
accidental: SubRetrv arises from CC's subagent summaries omitting exact code (Cross-Agent
SubRetrv is 50.15% of CC's SubRetrv and **does not exist in MSA at all**), from shell-based
editing that gives no feedback (Patch-Adjacent 17.96–26.47% in MSA vs 8.67% in CC), from
`cat` without line numbers triggering `grep -n` follow-ups (Within-Sequence 30.05% in MSA), and
from long debugging loops (Long-Distance 52.40% in MM3, whose average trajectory is 77.99
steps against 29.99–58.06). SimScrpt is 5.91–9.98x more frequent in MSA than in CC, and the
paper ties that to a specific prompt line — CC's built-in "NEVER create files unless they're
absolutely necessary" confines SimScrpt to ephemeral probes (81.03–93.45% of ephemeral
SimScrpt scripts are inspection probes) while MSA also regenerates file-based test and patch
scripts. ReTest comes from repository test-harness ignorance, truncated output, and reasoning
loops; Appendix A.2.4 shows MSAS46 "reruns the migrations test suite 22 times and
test_makemigrations_no_init 18 times without another patch until the step budget is exhausted
and the task fails". For the mitigation, the account is that **abstraction level is what
matters**: seven high-level principles ("persist and reuse artifacts rather than regenerate
similar variants") generalise, while 23–41 trace-specific rules ("For non-ASCII content in
shell, use heredoc syntax or a temp script file with 'PYTHONIOENCODING=utf-8' rather than
inline '-c' commands") do not. Evidence: **partially-evidenced** for the behaviours,
**asserted** for the abstraction mechanism. The behaviours are genuinely derived from data:
Table 2's scenario decomposition, Figures 3–5's frequency and AST functional breakdowns, and
Appendix A.1.1's disclosure that the LLM fallback handles 0.94% of CC steps but **31.17% of
MSAS46 steps** (7.26% of all steps) are real evidence. The *mitigation* mechanism is not
ablated at all: there is no arm that holds abstraction level fixed and varies specificity, and
no arm that varies the number of principles. The paper says "One possible mechanism is…",
which is the correct register, and the claim that human abstraction is the active ingredient is
supported only by a co-occurrence: seven general rules beat 23–41 specific rules.

**Alternative routes.**
1. **Remove the seven DevSkills and see how much of the 41.73% survives.** — pros: it is the
   single cheapest possible test, it directly targets the paper's own stated mechanism, and the
   paper supplies the natural leave-one-out: principle 5 (capture test output to a file,
   extract the signal, rerun only if the code changed) targets ReTest, and Table 3 shows
   CC — which has **no built-in instruction discouraging ReTest**, the one behaviour where CC
   shows no advantage at 2.09 executions per task — saved the *least* on Verified-200
   (−13.94%) despite the most headroom. That asymmetry is a prediction the ablation would test.
   cons: with 15 of 24 cells at n = 1 and a cost noise floor of 2.44%–8.35%, a seven-way
   leave-one-out at 8 configurations × 200 tasks is 56 single runs and would be read mostly
   through noise; the effects are not separable from Pass@1 movements (CC loses 1.50 pp on
   Verified-200); and there is a real chance the answer is "most of it is in one or two
   principles", which weakens the abstraction story without weakening the savings.
   why-not-chosen: the authors' budget framing is explicit — full replication is $7,500 and
   they bought 17 replicated cells with it. cost-to-test: ~$150–400 in agent API spend at the
   paper's own per-run costs, which is 5–12% of AXV's entire compute budget for a *proxy* on
   someone else's task set. **would-it-win: maybe** — it wins as science, and it is the only
   route that distinguishes "abstraction" from "seven specific rules that happen to be good".
2. **Change the prompt instead of adding a skill.** Every DevSkill is a prompt-level
   behavioural rule; the paper preloads them into the system prompt (Appendix A.4). — pros:
   DevSkills and SynSkills are already prompt interventions, so the "skills" framing may be
   adding nothing — the honest null is that seven good lines in a system prompt, delivered by
   a human who read 1,200 trajectories, beat 23–41 lines produced by a model reading the same
   1,200 trajectories, and the mechanism is human selection rather than any skill abstraction;
   this is a *framing* alternative rather than a rival method, and it does not change the
   budget, the seeds, or the task set, so it is not cheaper. The real test — hold the author
   constant and vary the abstraction level, e.g. a human who writes 7 rules about a task set
   they have *not* seen — is a genuine experiment and is not in the paper. why-not-chosen: not
   discussed; the authors treat skill format as settled and list it as a threat to validity
   ("Findings depend on our agent-synthesis pipeline and developer-skill design").
   cost-to-test: 2 arms × 8 configurations × 3 seeds. **would-it-win: maybe** — it is the
   experiment that would make the paper's headline mechanism a finding instead of a plausible
   story.
3. **Just do less.** The paper never tests the null intervention of reducing the per-task cost
   cap, raising the step limit, or narrowing the tool surface. — pros: it is the cheapest
   possible way to buy a similar dollar saving, it requires no new agent capability, and the
   paper's own cost data makes it look competitive: ReTest alone is 0.83%–5.39% of task cost
   and SimScrpt 1.02%–9.57%, and CC's *existing* prompt rules already suppress SimScrpt from
   51.33–68.00% down to 20.67% of tasks — so prompt-level suppression demonstrably works and
   the paper credits it only in passing. cons: a hard cap truncates work rather than removing
   waste, so it converts a cost problem into a Pass@1 problem, and the paper is explicit that
   DevSkills was chosen partly because it does not sacrifice task performance; it also changes
   the estimand from "cost to do the task" to "cost within budget", which is the same
   estimand slippage §5.3 of arXiv:2609.31381 warns about in a different paper.
   why-not-chosen: not tested at all, which is a gap rather than a choice. cost-to-test:
   1 arm × 8 configurations. **would-it-win: maybe** — plausibly buys half the saving for free,
   and would change what AXV should build.

**Evidence quality.** Seeds and spread: this is where the paper is strongest and where the
finding has to be read carefully. All 8 baselines were run **three times** and Table 7 reports
the full noise floor: Pass@1 SD **0.87 pp (CC/Verified) to 4.00 pp (MM3/Pro)**, cost CV
**2.44%–8.35%**, CoP CV up to **9.68%** and up to 3.45x wider than the cost floor because CoP
inherits Pass@1's variance in its denominator. 9 approach cells were also run three times
(Table 8) to validate transferring baseline noise to single-run cells; 8 of 9 show no
amplification (Pass@1 ratio 0.17–1.33x, cost ratio 0.30–1.38x), and the authors are
appropriately humble that "for three normal runs… an estimated s carries a relative standard
error of ≈52%". The one exception, CodeGraph + MSAQ35+ on Pro-100 at 2.58x cost noise, is
handled by using the larger observed floor and checking the effect survives. There is a
genuine safety-margin argument: every robust single-run effect stays robust under a 1.5x
inflation of its floor, and summing the residual sign-reversal probabilities over the 13
robust cost effects gives **0.07 expected reversals**. The decision rule is explicit —
σ_Δ = s√(1/R + 1/3), robust at |Δ|/σ_Δ ≥ 1.645, which reduces to **|Δ| ≥ 1.90s for a single
run**. Ablations: CodeGraph's mechanism is properly dissected in Table 4, which is the best
mechanistic work in the paper. The SynSkills amortisation analysis in Appendix A.4.1 is an
ablation that **cuts against the authors' own approach** and they report it. Baseline
strength: four configurations across two frameworks and four backbone models, with CC/S46 vs
MSA/S46 isolating framework and the three MSA cells isolating model — that is a real design.
Compute: not released. Unlike arXiv:2609.30721 and arXiv:2609.31381 there is no code or data
link in the paper, and the AI Use Statement discloses only that an LLM pipeline generates the
SynSkills. Contamination risk: SWE-bench Verified is human-filtered and Pro is public; the
RQ1/RQ2–RQ3 split is by creation date within repository, which is clean, but the skills are
*derived from* the RQ1 trajectories and then applied to Verified-200, and to Pro-100 across
benchmarks. That is a stated generalisation challenge and it is why the Pro numbers are weaker.
Evaluation breadth: two benchmarks, two frameworks, four models, 300 analysis tasks, 300
evaluation tasks, 17 of 24 cells at n = 1. **The single most likely way the claim is wrong:**
the headline is "developer-designed skills, roughly twice the maximum gain from
agent-synthesized skills", and that is a comparison between **a human's 7 principles tuned on
1,200 trajectories of exactly these two benchmarks** and **a model's 23–41 rules generated
from the same 1,200 trajectories**, with 15 of 24 cells at n = 1, no published token prices,
and — on the paper's own appendix arithmetic — most of the agent-synthesised advantage gone
once synthesis cost is charged. The result that survives all of this is narrower and more
useful: **a small number of human-authored behavioural rules measurably reduce agent cost
without measurably reducing Pass@1, and a much larger automatically generated rule set does
not.** That is a claim about the value of a human in the loop, not about the number 41.73%.

**Confidence.** **medium** — the behaviour analysis is well-derived and the noise-floor
discipline is better than most papers in this area, but the headline rests on 15 single-run
cells, unpublished prices, and a mechanism that is asserted rather than ablated.
Unknowns: (1) the token price schedule, without which no dollar figure is reproducible — the
only price datum in the paper is the 3x S46:H45 ratio; (2) the CoP formula, which is given only
as a name plus a citation, though the appendix's numbers are consistent with cost ÷ Pass@1;
(3) whether the DevSkills advantage survives at a scale where SynSkills amortise, which the
paper says depends on deployment scale and puts the crossover above 3,000 tasks; (4) the
abstraction-level mechanism, which no arm isolates; (5) the 2 unexplained CodeGraph failures on
Pro-100; (6) whether the 0.60 Jaccard threshold for SimScrpt, chosen by manually inspecting 20
pairs per configuration, transfers to other domains — the paper notes lower thresholds match
generic scaffolding; (7) the 2.09–5.39% of steps handled by the LLM fallback in Appendix
A.1.1, which is 31.17% for MSA/S46 and therefore a labelling-reliability concern for exactly
the configuration with the largest reported effect.

**Experiment.** **not-applicable** — and the reason is worth stating, because it is a
statement about what AXV's budget can and cannot buy.
- Harness that would be needed: an AXV-side skill experiment on the Quill/Lens/Loom loop —
  one arm with a developer-authored skill equivalent to DevSkills preloaded, one arm without,
  over a fixed task set, with per-run LLM API cost as the metric, the 1.90s robust threshold
  from the paper's own rule, and the baseline run three times to establish a noise floor before
  any single-run effect is read. That is a harness change to `dustin-dev-35/autoresearch` on a
  branch `experiment/2609.30725-skills`, which section 10.3 permits, and it does **not** need a
  GPU at all — the cost being measured is agent API spend.
- Why it was not run: the cost is in agent API spend, not GPU spend, and it is far outside
  $3.25. The paper's own arithmetic is the cleanest statement of the gap: a single
  configuration × benchmark cell at its observed per-run cost is $0.10–$1.13, so 8 cells at
  one seed is roughly $4–5 of API spend before any replication, and a properly powered version
  (8 baselines × 3 + 3 seeds per arm) is on the order of **$150–400** — 5–12% of AXV's entire
  lifetime compute budget spent proxying a result on someone else's task set. The board's
  batch plan for RunPod is also still `in_review` on AXV-4 with the seed count and mount type
  unresolved, and provisioning ahead of an unapproved plan is the failure mode §10.2 names.
- What AXV should do instead: the paper's transferable result is the *shape* of the seven
  principles, and AXV can act on that for $0.00 by writing its skills to that shape —
  high-level, trace-agnostic, one principle per diagnosed failure mode, preloaded in the
  system prompt. AXV already does this: all three of its agent instruction files are
  developer-authored behavioural principles derived from observed failure modes. The claim it
  actually needs to test is the cheap version — *does an AXV developer-written skill recover
  cost on the autoresearch harness*, measured in AXV's own agent spend, which AXV already
  records and can compare across batches at zero marginal cost.
- Baseline source: `experiments/leaderboard.jsonl`, 0 bytes at the time of this memo. No
  comparable record exists and nothing was re-run.
