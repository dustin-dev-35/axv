# Reading memo — 2609.30721v1 — When 10,000 Windows Are Not 10,000 Tests

**Claim.** Subject-disjoint splitting does not make sliding-window test rows independent, so
an IID interval on a paired model comparison is anti-conservative and must be replaced by a
session-centred Bartlett-HAC interval whose width grows with overlap
([arXiv:2609.30721](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30721)).

**What advanced.** The baseline is not a model but a *reporting practice*: reporting a paired
Accuracy-difference interval on pooled IID standard errors. The delta is on Figure 3(a),
Figure 4, Table IV and Table VI, and it is entirely in the uncertainty, not the point
estimate. At 75% overlap, controlled calibration gives **16.9% Type-I error for IID
observed-record inference against 7.2% for session-centred Bartlett-HAC**, against a nominal
5% — a 3.4x anti-conservatism reduced to 1.4x. In absolute width terms, Table IV reports the
HAC interval **1.22x–1.66x wider** than the IID interval at 75% overlap: WISDM 5 s
0.510 → 0.668 pp (1.31x), WISDM 10 s 0.743 → 0.906 pp (1.22x), HARTH 10 s
0.520 → 0.862 pp (1.66x). Nominal test-window counts grow **3.93x–3.97x** from 0% to 75%
overlap while variance-equivalent information grows only **1.75x–1.94x** (WISDM 5 s 1.94,
WISDM 10 s 1.87, HARTH 10 s 1.75). The paper's second, larger delta is that the choice of
*metric* flips the scientific conclusion: Table VI, HARTH 10 s at 75% overlap, paired
Accuracy-difference is **+0.07 pp [−0.36, +0.50]** (interval includes zero) while Macro-F1 is
**−13.21 pp [−16.03, −10.39]** favouring MiniROCKET. Compute: 1,200 out-of-fold predictions
from two classifier families (Random Forest on statistical+spectral features; MiniROCKET plus
ridge) on WISDM (51 subjects, 933 sessions) and HARTH (22 subjects, 393–409 sessions), plus
1,000–2,000 Monte-Carlo test sets per simulation condition. Eval protocol: subject-level
five-fold OOF, training windows at 50% overlap, frozen prediction files as the evaluation
object, inference run from matched paired prediction files. Flags: the metric-flip result
appears **only on HARTH**, at **one of two datasets**; WISDM favours MiniROCKET on both
metrics and all three targets, so the "metric changes the conclusion" claim is a
single-dataset result. The bandwidth is a **frozen default chosen using earlier synthetic
calibration that reused the same seeds** (§IV-C, stated by the authors: "The final historical
HAC recalculation reused the earlier simulation seeds… independence between bandwidth
selection and reported calibration" is not established). The paper's own new independent
calibration moves the numbers: IID 5.2/8.7/**16.9**% and HAC 5.5/6.4/**7.9**% at 0/50/75,
versus the historical 5.4/8.5/16.9% and 5.9/5.5/**7.2**%. A length-stress retest gives
HAC **6.7%** against the historical **8.85%**, a 2.12 combined-MCSE disagreement that the
paper retains as two separate outcomes rather than resolving.

**Mechanism.** The causal account is that overlapping windows share raw samples, so adjacent
predictions are mechanically correlated, and errors persist within a recording and a subject,
so the contrast sequence D_ijt = C^A − C^B is a dependent paired sequence rather than
independent draws. Nominal window count is therefore a row count, not an evidence count. The
paper converts this into a *rule*: declare the target population, choose the aggregation
rule, identify the highest independent unit, then pick the matching estimator —
session-centred HAC for the fixed-record target, a subject-clustered sandwich for new
sessions, paired inference across subjects for new subjects. Evidence: **partially-evidenced**.
The session-centring and bandwidth machinery is genuinely isolated and ablated — §V-B
decomposes W_HAC/W_IID into a centring ratio times a dependence ratio (0.83 × 1.11 = 0.92 for
WISDM 5 s, 0.81 × 1.03 = 0.84 for WISDM 10 s), and a Macro-F1 influence-function interval is
checked against a 1,999-replicate session-aware block bootstrap with near-identical results.
What is *not* demonstrated is the calibration of the frozen default bandwidth, and the paper
says so. The target-alignment result (Fig. 3b: IID 72.4%, within-session HAC 66.2%,
equal-subject 5.5% under subject/session heterogeneity) is asserted on a simulation whose
parameters are not released, so it cannot be independently checked from the text.

**Alternative routes.**
1. **Moving-block or stationary bootstrap instead of HAC on the window contrast.** — pros:
   resampling validity for stationary dependence is a weaker assumption than a correct lag
   structure; block length is one knob rather than a bandwidth-plus-centring choice; it
   extends to any metric, including Macro-F1, without a separate influence-function
   construction, which removes the largest single piece of machinery in the paper; it composes
   with subject-level resampling for the new-subject target. cons: the pointwise asymptotic
   validity rate is slower than the closed-form Bartlett estimator, so at the fixed-record
   target with few contributing sessions it can be materially anti-conservative — the paper
   itself reports (§IV-A) that "the original multistage hierarchical bootstrap is retained as a
   diagnostic because pilot calibration found it over-conservative for the fixed-record
   target", which is evidence against this route in exactly the regime AXV cares about; and
   the paper does not report a head-to-head block-bootstrap calibration at the fixed-record
   target, so "would be adequate here" is inference, not measurement. why-not-chosen: the
   authors needed a *closed-form* width for each of three targets so the audit can emit a
   comparable interval per row of Table II, and a bootstrap is a different kind of object;
   §IV-A's own pilot result gives them a concrete reason to prefer HAC. cost-to-test: one
   simulation arm at each overlap, M=2000, three seeds — about 15 minutes on the same CPU
   rig used here. **would-it-win: maybe** — likely wins on Macro-F1 and on the new-session
   target, plausibly loses on the fixed-record target where the paper already saw the
   bootstrap under-cover.
2. **Aggregate to the highest independent unit first, then do ordinary inference — skip the
   variance estimator entirely.** Instead of correcting window-level intervals, compute one
   number per session and one per subject and run a t-test or a cluster sandwich on those. —
   pros: this is the paper's own new-subject target, and my run shows it is by far the most
   robust arm in the whole design — equal-subject paired inference stayed at **0.023–0.044**
   Type-I across every overlap and every dependence strength in the balanced design, and at
   **0.032** under model-pair heterogeneity where IID hit 0.776; it is also the cheapest rule
   to state, the easiest to audit, and the one least dependent on bandwidth choices; it needs
   no assumption about the *shape* of within-session dependence, only on the count of
   clusters. cons: it throws away within-session information, so it is strictly less powerful
   per unit of compute — the paper's §III-C 1,999-replicate percentile bootstrap over 22
   subject clusters on HARTH is visibly noisy, and the paper itself notes in §VII that
   "HARTH's 22 clusters also limit the reliability of sandwich and percentile-bootstrap
   intervals"; with AXV's 3 seeds there are 3 clusters, which is below the point where any
   cluster-robust interval is trustworthy at all. why-not-chosen: inference, not fact. The
   authors want to answer the *fixed-record* question too ("which model is better on these
   recorded windows"), and aggregating to subjects does not answer it. cost-to-test: zero new
   compute; the estimator is already implemented in this run's `train.py` as the `subj` arm,
   and measured. **would-it-win: yes for AXV's actual protocol, no for the paper's stated
   scope** — and that split is the finding, not a hedge.
3. **More of something cheap: more independent units at the same cost.** AXV buys 3 seeds with
   its budget. Three *sessions* or three *checkpoints* from the same run are the same dollars
   and are not statistically independent in the way a new seed is. — pros: directly attacks the
   cluster count, which my run shows is the binding constraint (equal-subject inference is the
   only arm that never misbehaves, and its power is set by the number of clusters);
   checkpoints are free once the pod is up. cons: the same line of §VII applies — training-set
   randomness is not propagated across overlapping folds, and the paper explicitly declines to
   treat five OOF folds as five inference units ("Five OOF folds provide only a descriptive
   training-set stability check, not five independent inference units"); adjacent checkpoints
   are more autocorrelated than adjacent windows, so the correction is harder, not easier.
   why-not-chosen: not discussed by the paper; it is a budget question, not a statistics one.
   cost-to-test: one run writing per-checkpoint metrics. **would-it-win: no** — it buys
   correlated units and then pays the HAC correction on top, which is strictly worse than
   buying independent ones.

**Evidence quality.** Seeds and spread: the real-data analysis is run from *frozen* prediction
files, so there is no training-seed variance in the reported intervals at all — which removes
a source of noise and also removes the only way the result could be checked against a
different fold assignment. The simulation reports Monte-Carlo intervals and MCSE, and the
authors were careful to add an independently seeded post-review calibration after noticing the
seed reuse, which is unusually good practice and is reported against them. Ablations: genuine
and specific — centring vs dependence decomposition, Macro-F1 influence-function vs block
bootstrap, per-session bandwidth, HAC bandwidth grid, frozen 5 s WISDM length sensitivity,
common-session recomputation, class-level breakdown, and the nested-overlap purity check on
HARTH. Baseline strength: the estimators are classical (Newey–West 1987) and the paper is
explicit that its contribution is "the executable claim-to-analysis mapping, not a new generic
variance estimator"; Table I positions it against Binette–Reiter 2024, Hong et al. 2026 and
Anglin 2026, and honestly records "not shown" for each. That is the right posture and it lowers
the risk of a hidden negative result. Compute: modest, fully released
(`github.com/xinze8806-ship-it/sliding-window-confidence-audit`, v1.0.0), code + frozen
predictions + configs + reproduction scripts. Contamination risk: low — no LLM-generated
content, no pretraining-overlap question, subject-disjoint splits. Evaluation breadth: **two
datasets and two classifier families**, which the paper states in §VII as the main limitation.
**The single most likely way the claim is wrong:** the headline factors (16.9%, 7.2%,
1.75x–1.94x, 1.22x–1.66x) are all conditional on a data-generating process the authors do not
release, and my sweep shows the same estimator produces IID Type-I anywhere from 0.069 to
0.210 at 75% overlap depending on a persistence parameter the paper never states. The
*direction* is robust; the *numbers are not portable*, and a reader who quotes 16.9% as "the"
overlap penalty is quoting one point in a range the paper has not bounded.

**Confidence.** **medium** — the estimator, its ablation and its code release are solid and
the direction replicates on a parameter-free reimplementation, but every headline magnitude is
conditional on an unreleased simulation design and the metric-flip result is single-dataset.
Unknowns: (1) the simulation's subject count, session count, raw session duration, AR
coefficients and threshold construction; (2) whether the frozen default bandwidth was
genuinely chosen without looking at the reported calibration — the paper says it was not
established, and its own independent rerun moved 7.2% to 7.9% and 8.85% to 6.7%; (3) whether
the metric-flip result survives a third HAR dataset; (4) Macro-F1 at the fixed-record target
is computed by an influence function whose implementation I did not read; (5) no
training-seed replication exists for the real-data intervals, so their width has never been
shown to be stable under a different fold assignment; (6) per-session bandwidth is reported
as *not* curing residual miscalibration, and I did not test that independently.

**Experiment.** **verified (partially)** — the paper's operational claim is real and AXV
should adopt the rule, but AXV should not quote the paper's numbers.
- Harness: no RunPod pod. Verification run `verify-2609.30721-typei-20260928`, committed at
  `experiments/runs/verify-2609.30721-typei-20260928/`; `train.py` is self-contained on numpy
  and scipy. The claim is a statement about a variance estimator on binary paired outcomes and
  is not GPU-bound; provisioning the PRO 6000 MIG 24GB at $0.59/hr would have spent AXV budget
  to compute the same number more slowly. `harness_commit` is null and `diff.patch` is empty by
  design — no harness change was required and no `experiment/<arxiv-id>-<series>` branch was
  created. This is recorded as a deliberate deviation with its reason, not as a GPU result.
- Runs: 63 conditions = 3 overlaps × 5 persistence levels × 3 seeds, plus 2 heterogeneity arms
  × 2 persistence levels × 3 overlaps × 3 seeds, plus 15 information-growth conditions.
  M = 1,000 Monte-Carlo test sets per condition; 3 seeds (20260928/29/30), each an independent
  repetition of the whole study, not one draw from a single study.
- Spread: at rho = 0.99, 75% overlap — IID 0.199 / 0.218 / 0.212, HAC 0.081 / 0.080 / 0.087,
  equal-subject 0.027 / 0.039 / 0.029.

- Cost: **$0.00**. Total pod spend this heartbeat: $0.00. Budget remaining: **$3.25 of $3.25**.
- Caveat: the latent is Gaussian and thresholded, not a Bernoulli-AR process, so absolute
  Type-I values are conditional on that choice. Eight of eight pre-registered prediction
  checks hold; the surface is in `metrics.json` under `predictions.surface_iid_vs_hac`.
- The result AXV should act on: IID Type-I at 75% overlap ran **0.069 (rho=0) to 0.210
  (rho=0.99)** against nominal 0.05, while session-centred HAC ran **0.036 to 0.083** over the
  same surface, and the IID−HAC gap was non-negative at every overlap and every persistence
  level. Nominal row growth 3.97x; information growth 2.81x with mechanical overlap alone and
  2.62x at rho = 0.99 — so mechanical overlap costs 41% of the nominal information and AR(1)
  persistence adds only 7% more. **And the finding the paper does not make:** under a shared
  subject/session *difficulty* shift, IID Type-I was **0.059** — a shared difficulty shift
  moves both arms together and cancels in C_A − C_B. Only when the heterogeneity acts on the
  **model-pair difference** does target misalignment appear, and then it is severe: IID 0.776,
  HAC 0.800, equal-subject 0.032. Since AXV's `delta` is always a paired contrast, AXV is
  largely protected against the shared-difficulty form and exposed to the differential form.
  Concretely: AXV is safe when both arms see the same data, same seed family and same
  difficulty, and unsafe when something makes one arm systematically easier on a subset of
  the runs.
