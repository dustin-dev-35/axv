# Reading memo — 2609.31381v1 — Completed Pairs Hide Capped Failures

**Paper:** Completed Pairs Hide Capped Failures: A ReVerPi Case Study of Selective Context Projection - Guangzhe Zhang, [arXiv:2609.31381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31381v1)

**Claim.** When a runner suppresses an arm's companion whenever the first arm fails to
complete, reporting only completed pairs turns a large capability difference into an exact
tie, and the fix is procedural rather than statistical: execute both allocated arms
independently of the first arm's completion
([arXiv:2609.31381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31381)).

**What advanced.** The baseline is the standard agent A/B practice of reporting only the pairs
where both arms finished. The delta is on Table 3, Table 4 and §5.2. Completed pairs give
**12/15 correct for full and 12/15 for projected** — an exact tie. Restoring all 27 realized
intervention boundaries and treating an observed cap as a known zero gives
projected-minus-full success bounded in **[−9, +1] tasks over 27 runs, i.e. [−33.3, +3.7]
percentage points**. In absolute terms: full has 14 known successes and 7 unexecuted
companions, so its success count is 14–21; projected has 12 known successes and 3 unexecuted,
so its count is 12–15; the contrast runs from 12−21 = −9 to 15−14 = +1. The mechanism is
stated as a probability identity, §3.3 Eq. 2: **Pr(M_i2 = 1 | C_i1 = 0, A_i1, X_i) = 0** — the
companion is unobserved *with probability one* in the capped region, so observation positivity
fails and no adjustment can recover it. Compute: an 86-run source-reading campaign on ReVerPi
(a Pi 0.84.2 extension) with 641 model requests, 7,971,276 input and 231,556 output tokens,
8,202,832 total, 74.9% of input reported cached; provider-returned model `deepseek-flash`,
effort low, concurrency 1, a fixed 12-suffix-request cap per arm. Eval protocol: matched
common-prefix replay — the tape is sealed at the first eligible dispatch and both arms replay
it, so the first suffix request of each arm matches in tools, model settings and every field
except the declared tool-result representation. Flags: this is **n = 27 runs from a single
author, a single model label, and a single stochastic continuation per arm**. There is no
replication anywhere. Tasks were investigator-authored over allowlisted Python package
sources, and the paper states the experimental unit is "a submitted run at one realized
prefix, rather than an API request or an independent sample implied by a new seed". The two
arms are matched, which is the strong part; the model is a proxy-reported label that "does not
authenticate backend weights", and the private price schedule and bill are unavailable, so
the paper reports token categories and "algebraic sensitivity rather than dollar savings" —
**no dollar figure is claimed anywhere in the paper**.

**Mechanism.** The causal account is that completion is itself an outcome and is
outcome-dependent on the first arm, so filtering on completion is conditioning on a
post-treatment variable that the intervention affects. The paper backs this with three
separate pieces of machinery rather than one: a worst-case partial-identification bound
(Manski), Appendix A.3's proof that observation adjustment cannot supply the absent companions
(Eq. 13, H̄_D − H̄ = Cov_N(H, D)/D̄, with a concrete exclusion in the record: the observed
selected cap has H_i = 1 and D_i = 0), and a principal-stratification argument that the
eleven jointly correct pairs are *fully* identified within the frame because all 16 non-members
have an observed zero. Evidence: **demonstrated** for the censoring arithmetic and
**partially-evidenced** for the resource claims. The arithmetic is not an assertion: the bounds
are sharp and attained, and my independent recomputation from the paper's Table 3 counts
reproduced [−9, +1] tasks and [−33.3, +3.7] pp exactly (leaderboard record
`verify-2609.31381-mcnemar-20260928:paper-recheck`, `matches_paper: true`). The resource result
is where the labels matter: on the J = 11 jointly correct stratum, logical tokens are
1,267,036 full vs 949,774 projected, a sum ratio of **0.750, i.e. 25.0% less in aggregate** —
but the geometric mean is **0.883**, the median is **1.292**, the arithmetic mean of ratios is
**1.113**, and **seven of eleven pairs increase**. Remove the single largest saving
(`more-itertools-recipes`) and the sum ratio becomes **1.077**. Suffix requests go the other
way: **55 projected vs 35 full, a 57% increase**, with projected using more requests in eight
pairs, fewer in two, and the same in one. The token *categories* move the right way for cost
(−407,680 cached, +89,684 uncached) but the modelled monetary difference
(89,684·p_u − 407,680·p_c + 734·p_o)/10⁶ has a **sign that depends on unpublished prices**. So
the aggregate saving is real and the per-pair effect is not: the headline 25% is one case.

**Alternative routes.**
1. **Run both arms unconditionally and budget the batch so the second arm always fits.** —
   pros: this is the paper's own §6 recommendation and it is strictly more informative than
   any statistical repair, because it converts an unknown into an observation; it needs no
   estimator, no bounds, and no positivity argument; it makes the `experiment_runs` row
   self-describing, because a terminated arm is recorded as terminated rather than silently
   absent. cons: it costs money. AXV's board-set total is **$3.25 for everything, all time**,
   and at $0.59/hr that is **5 h 31 m of pod time company-wide**; a batch where every seed
   pays for two complete arms instead of one-and-a-bit cannot be run three times, so the fix
   trades directly against the seed count AXV is already short on (see the Experiment section:
   80% power against a 10-point difference needs n = 217). The paper's own history shows the
   failure is not hypothetical: of 27 boundary runs, **ten first arms exhausted their cap and
   all ten companions were never executed**, so the cost of the fix is precisely the cost of
   the arms that were being skipped. why-not-chosen: the author had a per-phase wall limit of
   3,600 s and a 12-request cap, and the campaign was already built around a sequential
   single-concurrency runner. cost-to-test: the change is a scheduling rule, not code that
   needs proving; the AXV-specific cost is that n drops. **would-it-win: yes** — it is the
   only route that makes the number mean what it appears to mean.
2. **Budget-terminate the whole pair and report the pair count, not the arm count.** — pros:
   zero extra dollars; the censoring becomes *symmetric*, so a terminated pair removes a
   comparison rather than one side of it, and the estimand becomes "success within budget",
   which is a legitimate and arguably more operationally honest quantity; it needs no
   allocation-rule change at all, only a reporting change, and it is compatible with AXV's
   fixed wall-clock budget. cons: it still loses the comparisons, and it is not what AXV's
   `delta` column currently means, so it changes the schema; it converts a *known* gap
   (−9 tasks, fully bounded) into an *unbounded* one, which is a real loss of information;
   and with AXV's n = 3, dropping a pair leaves 2 and the exact paired test at n = 2 cannot
   reach the 0.05 level either (minimum attainable two-sided p = 0.50). why-not-chosen: the
   paper is explicitly against it — §3.3's point is that suppression is *outcome-dependent*,
   and a symmetric budget kill still conditions on a resource that the intervention moves
   (projection changed suffix requests from 35 to 55, so the projected arm is more likely to
   be the one cut). cost-to-test: a schema change and one page of the run record.
   **would-it-win: no** — it fixes the asymmetry and leaves the censoring.
3. **Report the bound, not the estimate.** Keep the current runner and emit the finite-frame
   interval [−9, +1] instead of a point tie. — pros: it requires **no new compute and no
   allocation change**, so it costs nothing against a $3.25 budget; it is exactly correct for
   the frame that was actually run; it is the paper's own primary result; and it is the only
   route that composes with a budget that cannot buy the arms. cons: an interval spanning 10
   tasks is not actionable, so it changes what AXV can conclude without changing what AXV
   knows — the memo and the post would say "unknown", which is honest and commercially
   useless; it is easy to launder into a headline as "no significant difference", which is
   exactly the failure the paper is about; and the bound is only sharp for a *fixed recorded
   frame*, not for a population, so it must not be reported with a confidence level.
   why-not-chosen: not the author's objection — this is AXV's own constraint. AXV cannot
   currently afford the alternative, and the bound is the correct thing to publish in the
   meantime. cost-to-test: zero. **would-it-win: yes as an interim, no as a destination.**

**Evidence quality.** Seeds and spread: **none**. The paper says so repeatedly and precisely —
"Each arm provides one stochastic continuation", "with adaptive task construction and a
single stochastic continuation per arm, it is not an identified population survivor-average
causal effect under future resampling", and "source labels do not establish independence".
There is no variance estimate for any single-arm outcome anywhere in the paper, and the one
sign test it does run (seven token increases out of eleven, p = 0.55) is explicitly labelled "a
descriptive reference because tasks share source groups and the frame was adaptively
assembled". Ablations: the fitting/evaluation split is a real ablation and it is the most
valuable thing in the paper. Table 4 separates the four fitting pairs from the thirteen
outside-fitting runs: on 17 comparable runs the frozen selector and always-full **tie at 3
failures each**; outside fitting the selector incurs **one extra failure (3 vs 2) and
+122,682 logical tokens, +8.6%**. The overall tie is sustained by one fitting case,
`packaging-compatibility`, where projected is correct and full is wrong. The strict-format
sensitivity (Appendix C) adds exactly +1 failure to each arm — 3→4 full, 5→6 projected,
3→4 selector — **and does not change the ordering**, which is a genuinely good robustness check.
Leave-one-source-out gives 12 of 14 folds choosing the same threshold 95,519, but the
concatenated held-out choices incur **four failures against three** for the frozen rule.
Baseline strength: always-full and always-projected are the right controls, and Table 11
reports all four policies on the same 17 runs (always-full 3 failures / 1,823,900 logical
tokens; always-projected 5 / 1,751,145, −4.0%; frozen threshold 3 / 1,840,140, +0.9%;
hindsight threshold 2 / 1,787,343, −2.0%). Compute: fully released — project code at
`github.com/timwhitez/ReVer_Pi`, and the source archive ships an `anc/` directory with
analysis data, reproduction scripts, `data/analysis_v3.json`, `data/census.json`,
`data/quota_signal_evidence.json` and a `MANIFEST_SHA256.json`, with 22 named ancillary files.
Contamination risk: **not applicable** — no benchmark, no pretraining data,
investigator-authored questions over installed package sources. Evaluation breadth: one agent
harness (Pi 0.84.2), one read-only source-reading adapter, one proxy-reported model,
17 source labels, 27 boundary runs, tasks with two questions per source and deliberately
repeated histories. **The single most likely way the claim is wrong:** the bound [−9, +1] is
sharp and correct, but it is a statement about **one recorded campaign with one stochastic
continuation per arm and eleven source groups**, and the paper's own Appendix F shows what
that buys — the historical calibration has eleven rows in nine source groups with zero selected
harms, and the one-sided 95% upper bound for zero events in nine groups is 0.283, not 0. A
reader who takes "the selector is 8.6% worse outside fitting" as a property of the method
rather than a property of thirteen adaptively developed runs is over-reading it. The
procedural recommendation (execute both arms) is robust to all of this; the effect sizes are
not.

**Confidence.** **medium** — the censoring arithmetic is demonstrated, independently
recomputed, and the procedural fix is right; but every effect size rests on 27 adaptive runs
with no replication, and the headline 25% aggregate saving is one case.
Unknowns: (1) within-arm variance, which is nowhere estimated; (2) whether the true
projected-minus-full is anywhere in [−9, +1] — the bound is attained only under a frame that
already contains a known-bad case (`pathspec-util`); (3) the true model behind
`deepseek-flash`; (4) the price schedule, so the sign of the monetary effect is unknown even
though the category counts are published; (5) whether the `pathspec-util` cap is a property of
projection or of a bug — the paper traces it to an extension/runtime mismatch where the
recovery tool returned `isError: true` but the pinned Pi loop only marks failure when
`execute` throws, so **the refusal payloads were recorded with `isError=false`**, fixed
afterwards in PR 15, and the historical trajectories are unchanged; a second capped single-arm
run (`pathspec-match`, 244,370 tokens, partial) sits in the same source group; (6) whether 44
of 76 runs finishing before eligibility means the eligible cases are systematically harder —
the paper flags this tension and does not resolve it.

**Experiment.** **verified** — the AXV-specific half of the question is answered with a number.
- Harness: no RunPod pod. Verification run `verify-2609.31381-mcnemar-20260928`, committed at
  `experiments/runs/verify-2609.31381-mcnemar-20260928/`. The question is exact binomial
  arithmetic, so no accelerator is involved. `harness_commit` null, `diff.patch` empty by
  design, no `experiment/<arxiv-id>-<series>` branch.
- Runs: 6 power conditions plus a test-size check and a paper recheck. Exact two-sided McNemar
  on complete pairs, Monte-Carlo conditional on the discordant count, α = 0.05.
- **Minimum n for 80% power on a complete-case paired comparison:** a 10-point difference with
  arms at p = 0.90 vs 0.80 needs **n = 217**; 20 points at 0.90 vs 0.70 needs **n = 72**; 40
  points at 0.90 vs 0.50 needs **n = 26**; and a 10-point difference is *harder* as the base
  rate falls — **n = 315** at 0.80 vs 0.70, **n = 379** at 0.70 vs 0.60, **n = 412** at 0.60 vs
  0.50. For 50% power the 10-point figure is **n = 119**; for 20% power, **n = 51**.
- **The finding that answers AXV-9 directly:** at the n = 3 an AXV batch can afford, the exact
  two-sided paired test **cannot reach the 0.05 level at all** — the minimum attainable
  two-sided p-value with 3 pairs is **0.25**, and the empirical rejection rate under the null
  was **0.0000**. It is not a weak test, it is a test that cannot fire. Censoring is the
  smaller half of the problem: at n = 3 with one one-sided censored pair, a true difference is
  attenuated by at most a factor of 2/3, but that only matters once the test can reject, which
  it cannot.
- **Smallest change to the allocation rule:** stop gating the companion on the first arm's
  completion, and separately make the pairing budget-aware so a termination kills the pair
  rather than one side. The first is the paper's fix and it is free in code and expensive in
  dollars; the second is free in dollars and loses the comparison. Under AXV's $3.25, the
  defensible choice is the first for the *design* of the harness plus the third alternative
  route (report the bound) for what is published until the budget can pay for the second arm.
