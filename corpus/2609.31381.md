# Reading memo — 2609.31381v1 — Completed Pairs Hide Capped Failures: A ReVerPi Case Study of Selective Context Projection

**Claim.** A paired A/B that reports only the arms which both completed converts a bounded failure into an absent record, so a 12/15-versus-12/15 tie across completed pairs conceals a 27-run frame in which projected-minus-full success is bounded in [−9, +1] tasks. ([arXiv:2609.31381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31381))

**What advanced.** The baseline is the report the completed-pair view produces, which is the standard presentation for an A/B with a per-run resource cap.

- **The filter, §5.1 and Figure 2.** 86 submitted runs, 641 completed model requests, 8,202,832 reported tokens (7,971,276 input of which 5,974,272 cached = 74.9%; 231,556 output). 44 paired/capture runs finish before the projection-eligibility rule ever fires, 5 stop in capture, and 27 reach a verified intervention boundary. Of the 27, **12 stop**; in **10 of 10** first-arm request caps the companion was never executed.
- **The bounded contrast, Table 3.** Over all 27 boundary runs: full 14 correct / 6 known bounded failure / 7 unexecuted; projected 12 / 12 / 3. Full's success count is 14–21, projected's is 12–15, so projected-minus-full lies in **[−9, +1] tasks out of 27**. The completed-pair view reports a tie; the full frame cannot exclude projection losing nine tasks.
- **The selector's tie is a fitting artefact, Table 4.** On the 17 runs where both bounded outcomes are known, always-full and the frozen selector both record 3 failures. Split by fitting participation: the 4 fitting pairs give 1 full failure vs 0 selector failures, the 13 runs outside fitting give **2 vs 3** — one extra failure — and the logical-token totals go 1,419,197 → **1,541,879, i.e. +8.64%**. Over all 17 the token change is +0.89% (1,823,900 → 1,840,140). The 3/17 tie is sustained by one fitting case, `packaging-compatibility`, where projected is correct and full is wrong.
- **Cost on the fully ascertained stratum, §5.4 and Table 5.** The 11 jointly correct pairs are identified with certainty: 1,267,036 logical tokens for full against 949,774 for projected, ratio **0.750** (25.0% less in aggregate), but the geometric mean is 0.883 and the **median is 1.292**, and 7 of 11 pairs increase. Suffix requests go 35 → 55, **+57%**. Category deltas: +89,684 uncached input, −407,680 cached, +734 output.
- **The aggregate is one task, §5.4 and Figure 3.** Removing `more-itertools-recipes` (418,364 full tokens against 35,826 projected, Table 7) flips the sum ratio from 0.750 to **1.077**. The paper keeps the case and says so.
- **The mechanism case, §5.5 and Table 8.** `pathspec-util` is the omitted, selector-chosen projected continuation: full answers in 3 suffix requests and 84,202 suffix tokens; projected spends 12 requests and 237,629 tokens (**2.82×**) with no answer, after a successful archive search and then two exact reads refused by the shared 3-call quota. Those refusals were recorded with `isError=false` because the extension returned `isError: true` in its result object while the pinned Pi loop marks failure only on a throw; the fix landed afterwards in pull request 15 and the recorded trajectories are unchanged.
- **Compute and protocol.** 86 agent runs, one read-only adapter over Python package sources, one proxy-reported model (`deepseek-flash`), effort `low`, concurrency 1, 12/12 capture and suffix request caps, 131,072/65,536 context and output reservation, 3,600 s per-phase wall limit. Minutes of tokens, no GPU. Artifact and campaign archive published with SHA-256 `2b57e318…22f29c`, 12,980 listed artifacts, 73 of 76 runs passing the source-level auditor.

**Mechanism.** Two distinct causal accounts, with different standing.

- *Why the companion is missing.* The runner executes branches sequentially and breaks when a branch's state is not `completed`; a request-cap exhaustion leaves the state not completed while a wrong answer does not, so first-arm caps deterministically suppress companion execution. Because the suppression is a deterministic function of the first arm's endpoint, in the capped-first-arm region the probability of ever observing the companion is exactly zero: adjustment for the first endpoint is not merely hard, it is impossible, and inverse-observation weighting divides by zero. — evidence: **demonstrated**. This is a control-flow fact read off the recorded controller, confirmed by the counts (10/10 first-arm caps have no companion), and Appendix A.3 gives the algebra. The positivity failure is structural, not statistical.
- *Why projection costs more work.* Removing a passage forces a search and then an exact read, and both draw on a combined three-call quota, so the continuation can run out of recovery budget after it has already succeeded at retrieving. — evidence: **partially-evidenced**. The `pathspec-util` trace establishes retrieval success followed by refusal, but the paper states plainly that "neither the sufficiency of the retrieved fragments nor the outcome of a hypothetical larger quota" is established, and the error-flag defect means the recorded model-visible text was a bare `ReVer halted: recovery_quota` line with no error semantics. An alternative reading — that the agent would have kept flailing regardless, or that a fourth read would have sufficed — is not excluded by anything in the record.

**Alternative routes.**

1. **Fix the runner, keep the campaign.** Give each arm its own pre-authorised reservation and an independent execution decision, so a first-arm cap no longer suppresses its companion. Mechanism: the missingness is created by the scheduler, so removing the scheduler's coupling removes the missingness. Pros: the paper names this as its first design lesson and it is a small change to one loop; every unresolved coordinate becomes a known one, which collapses [−9, +1] toward a point estimate and eliminates the entire class of bound; it costs no new theory, no new model, no new data, and nothing about the projection mechanism is disturbed, so the paper's other results survive intact. Cons: it is **not** a re-analysis — it needs the 27 boundary runs re-executed, with new provider calls and new randomness, and the raw archive is not distributed, so the new frame is not comparable to the old one; the fix does not raise the budget, so a re-run is exposed to the same cap and may censor again, which means the reservation has to be generous enough to absorb a cap and that changes what is being measured; and the re-run inherits a model the paper cannot authenticate, since the label is proxy-reported. **Why-not-chosen** (stated by the authors, §6): the paper's object was the *existing* frame's identification problem, and the archived record could not be re-executed. **Cost to test:** 27 boundary runs' worth of provider tokens, on the order of a few dollars of API spend and no pod. **Would-it-win: yes**, trivially and by construction.

2. **Report only the fully ascertained stratum and drop the contrast.** Mechanism: the 11 jointly correct pairs are identified without any assumption, because every one of the 16 non-members has at least one observed zero, so no assignment to an unexecuted companion can move a run into or out of the stratum. Pros: this is the one estimand in the paper with zero missingness, so it needs no bounds, no positivity argument and no monotonicity assumption; it is the only result here that would survive a skeptical referee intact; and it still answers a real question, which is what projection costs when it works. Cons: the stratum is selected on the intervention itself — eligibility needs two completed full-observation requests and at least 10 KiB of repeatedly exposed content, and 44 of 76 runs never reached a boundary at all — so it is exactly the runs with the most history and plausibly the most retrieval work; reporting it alone invites the reader to read a within-success cost ratio as an end-to-end effect; and it silently discards the 12 runs where the intervention actually hurt, which *is* the finding. **Why-not-chosen** (inference): a methodological case study whose thesis is about selection cannot then restrict itself to the selected part. **Cost to test:** zero; the stratum is already fully enumerated in Tables 7 and 10. **Would-it-win: yes** as a clean secondary result, **no** as the headline.

3. **Publish cost-only accounting with a completion bit, and make no success claim.** Mechanism: the cost side is where the record is dense — 641 requests, 8.2M tokens, 12,980 artifacts, and three summaries that already disagree in a way that matters (sum 0.750, geometric 0.883, median 1.292). Pros: it needs no estimand debate; a report that says "25.0% fewer tokens in aggregate, +57% requests, median pair 29% *more* tokens, and 12 of 27 runs never completed" is publishable on the existing record and is arguably the more useful artefact for an engineer. Cons: costs in the 12 stopped pairs are expenditures without an answer, so cost-per-success is undefined there and the per-task cost number silently changes estimand depending on whether caps are counted; the aggregate is fragile to one case, as the leave-one-out flip to 1.077 shows; and it drops the paper's sharpest contribution, which is about *which runs you get to see*, not about price. **Why-not-chosen** (inference): the author is making an argument about estimands, so a cost-only paper would not carry it. **Cost to test:** zero. **Would-it-win: maybe** — publishable, but it answers the cheaper question.

**Evidence quality.** Seeds: **one stochastic continuation per arm**, by the paper's own statement, and no seed replication anywhere. For an A/B whose headline is a 12/15-versus-12/15 tie, that is the binding limitation, not the bounds. Ablations: genuinely strong for a case study. Table 11 compares always-full (3 failures), always-projected (5), the frozen threshold (3) and a hindsight oracle (2, with 2.0% less logical expenditure), and 14 leave-one-source-out folds choose the same threshold in 12 of 14 with held-out predictions giving 4 failures against the frozen rule's 3. Appendix C adds a strict whole-object scoring contract that moves 3/5/3 to 4/6/4 without changing the ordering, and exhibits a concrete decoder counterexample, `{"a":0,"a":1}`. Baselines: always-full is the right baseline for the policy question. But the *system* is compared to nothing — LLMLingua, Selective Context, observation masking and summarisation are all cited and none is run through this harness, so the paper cannot say whether ReVerPi is a good context-projection design, only how to evaluate one. Compute: trivial, 86 runs. Not a constraint on anything. Data: the weakest dimension. Tasks are investigator-authored on installed package subsets, often two questions per source; the appendix states repeatedly that "source labels do not establish independence"; model identity is proxy-reported and "does not authenticate backend weights"; the read-only adapter is not the complete Pi product; and raw requests, session databases, provider configuration and invoices are not distributed, so no dollar figure is derivable and no independent replay is possible. Breadth: one harness (Pi 0.84.2), one model, one task family, 27 boundary runs. **The single most likely way this claim is wrong: a reader takes "[−9, +1] means projection is roughly fine" instead of "[−9, +1] means the completed-pair tie was manufactured by the filter."** The width of that interval is exactly the 10 unexecuted arms, and 7 of them are *full* arms that were never run — so the lower end is mostly a statement about what the runner withheld, not about how projection behaves. The paper is careful about this and a summary will not be. Second-most-likely: the reverse error. Because 44 of 76 runs never reached a boundary, the 27 that did are a frame selected by an eligibility rule that requires large, repeatedly exposed observations, and the paper says this "favors trajectories that have already incurred repeated input" — so the 27 may be the worst case for projection by construction, and the [−9, +1] interval is not a bound on any deployment population.

**Confidence.** medium — the accounting is arithmetically checkable from the paper's own tables and AXV re-derived every quantity with zero mismatches, and the missingness mechanism is a control-flow fact; but the campaign is one model, one harness, one task family, one continuation per cell, adaptively constructed, with the raw archive withheld and the model identity unauthenticated. Unknowns: whether the stop rule, rather than projection, is what produced the observed token increase; whether `pathspec-util` would have succeeded with a larger quota or with the error flag correctly set, which the paper cannot say because the fix landed after the campaign; whether any of this transfers to a harness where projection is a model-side decision rather than a gateway-side one; the true billing, since the price schedule and invoice are withheld, so the "no dollar savings reported" line is a real limitation and not a stylistic choice; and whether the completed-pair tie would have been a tie at all had the companion run, which is exactly the quantity the record cannot supply.

## The question AXV-9 asked

> when an arm in the autoresearch harness is terminated for budget rather than for failure, what is the smallest change to our allocation rule that stops us reporting a null result that is really a censored one — and what is the smallest n at which our current protocol can distinguish "no difference" from "we stopped looking"?

**Part one — the smallest change, and it is smaller than a rule change.** It is one column and one write. AXV's `leaderboard.jsonl` schema has `metric_value` but nothing that distinguishes *a measured zero* from *a run that hit the wall clock*. Every terminated arm currently writes a value. The paper's entire contribution is that this is the bug.

The minimal correct allocation rule, in the paper's terms:

1. **A budget-terminated run records its own endpoint, not a metric.** Set `metric_value` to null and write a separate `endpoint` field with the reason (`budget_exhausted`, `pod_terminated`, `wall_clock_cap`). Null is not a gap in the record; it is the finding.
2. **Never let one arm's endpoint suppress the other.** This is the paper's §6 first lesson, and it is the part that generalises: AXV currently sizes pods per *batch*, not per *arm*, so one arm hitting the wall clock can end the batch. Per-arm reservations with independent stop decisions are the fix, and the paper's Appendix A.3 says exactly why a post-hoc fix is impossible: once the companion is not run, its probability of ever being observed is zero, and no amount of modelling recovers it.
3. **Separate a bounded failure from an unexecuted arm in the type system, and never average across them.** A bounded failure is a real zero. An unexecuted arm is a missing value. AXV's harness must be able to say which, and a `delta` that pools the two is reporting a censored result as a null.
4. **Never compute a delta from fewer than both arms' endpoints.** If either arm is unexecuted, there is no delta — only a bound, or a note.

**Part two — the smallest n. There is no such n, and the paper proves it for AXV's exact situation.**

The sharp finite-frame bound is `unexecuted arms − 1 ≤ |projected − full| ≤ unexecuted arms`, in task units. So the question "is the difference zero?" is *never* answered by a finite frame if any arm was not run: a single unexecuted arm leaves the bound at [−1, +1], which contains zero, so the honest answer is always "cannot distinguish". AXV can only distinguish "no difference" from "we stopped looking" by **not stopping looking**, or by accepting a bound and reporting it as a bound.

Concretely, for a two-arm comparison over N paired tasks with both arms completed, the answer is a power calculation, not a threshold. And the paper supplies the number that makes this sharp for AXV: the single largest saving in its whole campaign was one task, and removing it flipped the aggregate from 0.750 to 1.077. So a 1-task difference out of 11 jointly successful pairs was decisive for the headline. **AXV's own deltas are single-digit percentages on a handful of runs, and 1 task out of 11 was decisive in a published campaign with 641 model requests behind it.** Any n small enough for a $3.25 budget will produce differences of that order, and any of them can be carried by one arm.

**What AXV should do instead of picking an n.** Adopt a pre-registered minimum that is a function of the effect, not a constant:

- Fix in advance that a comparison whose interval spans a **pre-registered practical-equivalence margin** is reported as inconclusive, not as a null — the paper's §6, "Intervals crossing zero do not establish equivalence", and its Appendix F zero-event bound, where 0 events in 11 rows gives an upper value of about 0.283.
- Publish the margin before the run, in the leaderboard record, so the choice cannot be made after seeing the delta.
- Accept that under a $3.25 cap most comparisons will be inconclusive, and say so. An honest "inconclusive at this budget" is a better artefact than a null that a single task could have flipped.
- And fix the runner first, so that "inconclusive because we stopped looking" stops appearing at all. At $3.25 with 5.5 hours of total pod time, the two arms of one comparison cost the same whether or not the first one completed; **the cheapest available correctness win in AXV's whole experiment programme is to stop letting the first arm's wall clock decide the second arm's existence.**
