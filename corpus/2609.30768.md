# Reading memo — 2609.30768v1 — Does Thinking Help Fairness? Reasoning Tokens Resolve Some Biases but Create More

**Paper:** Does Thinking Help Fairness? Reasoning Tokens Resolve Some Biases but Create More - Deng Pan et al., [arXiv:2609.30768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30768v1) — Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: EMNLP 2026

**Authors:** Deng Pan, Joe Germino, Yihong Ma, Elizabeth Daly, Nuno Moniz, Ting Hua, Nitesh Chawla (7)

**Claim.** Across all nine (model, dataset) settings, enabling the thinking phase in three
32B-class reasoning models raises the counterfactual flip rate on Adult, COMPAS and Credit
in every case, and converts more baseline-agreeing pairs into counterfactual disagreements
than it converts disagreements back, by roughly 5x in aggregate.
([arXiv:2609.30768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30768))

**Ledger-row note.** The triage row for this paper was carried in the work queue, not read
back from Supabase `triage_ledger`, and at the time of writing no Supabase tool surface is
exposed to this run (see AXV-19). The row is treated as **provisional**. The arXiv ID,
version, title, authors, and the 5x figure in the row all match the paper's abstract exactly,
so the row is accurate as far as it goes; it is the record that is missing, not the content.

**What advanced.** The advance is a measurement, not a number. The prior literature holds two
camps with opposite verdicts on the same question, and this paper shows both camps are
reading different metrics off the same systems.

- **The control is genuinely good, and this is the paper's real strength.** The
  within-model, within-prompt paired ablation holds the model, the quantisation, the
  template and the record fixed, and varies only whether the thinking phase runs. Every
  prior result in the mitigation camp (Kabra et al. 2025) and the amplification camp
  (Shaikh et al. 2023, Wu et al. 2025, Moore et al. 2024, Karvonen and Marks 2025) is a
  cross-model or cross-prompt comparison. This is not.
- **The metric split, Table 9.** Counterfactual flip rate `D_cf` rises under thinking in
  **9 of 9** cells, absolutely: Qwen3-Adult 0.021 → 0.079 (+0.058), Qwen3-COMPAS
  0.029 → 0.069 (+0.040), Qwen3-Credit 0.005 → 0.021 (+0.016), QwQ-Adult 0.032 → 0.090
  (+0.058), QwQ-COMPAS 0.003 → 0.070 (+0.067), QwQ-Credit 0.025 → 0.120 (+0.095),
  R1-Adult 0.020 → 0.133 (+0.113), R1-COMPAS 0.003 → 0.125 (+0.122), R1-Credit 0.002 →
  0.097 (+0.095). Demographic-parity gap `D_group` over the same runs moves by at most
  0.017 in absolute value and **changes sign across datasets**: −0.006, −0.009, 0.000,
  −0.017, +0.034, +0.005, −0.002, +0.034, +0.014. The same 32B models, the same thinking
  tokens, the same records, read one way "thinking helps" (QwQ-Adult) and another way
  "thinking hurts" (QwQ-COMPAS). Settling the debate requires saying which metric is the
  fairness criterion, and the paper's answer is that counterfactual fairness is the one that
  moves consistently.
- **The group-level blind spot, Tables 3, 10, 11.** `T^α ≈ T^α'` to within 0.014
  (Qwen3-32B), 0.017 (QwQ-32B) and 0.012 (R1-Distill) on every entry, while the pair-level
  cell-c tilt `asym(c)` reaches 0.53 on QwQ-COMPAS (Table 5). A demographic-parity audit
  reads the marginal and is structurally unable to see the joint.
- **Compute and protocol.** 900,000 forward passes stated in §6 (3 models × 3 datasets ×
  5,000 pairs × 2 sides × 10 depths), plus 90,000 generations for the two arms. 4-bit AWQ,
  A100 80 GB, greedy decoding T=0. Adult, COMPAS and Credit, N=5,000 sampled per dataset,
  rendered to prose by a deterministic template, protected attribute flipped in one
  dictionary field. Effective N falls to 1,430 on R1-Distill-COMAS because the non-thinking
  arm refuses to answer on 72% of records.
- **Flags.** The gain is not on the authors' own benchmark — Adult, COMPAS and Credit are
  the field's standard tabular fairness set. It is not only at one scale: Appendix D
  replicates on gemma-2-27B and Llama-3.1-8B, off the Qwen lineage. It is not measured
  against a weak baseline: the baseline is the same model with the phase disabled. But the
  headline **is** on a metric of the authors' construction (cell c and cell b are defined in
  §3.2 by this paper), and the headline **is** a pooled figure presented as a per-cell
  invariant, which §5 shows it is not.

**Mechanism.** The paper offers two causal accounts with very different standing, and the
split matters more than either half.

- *Why the created/resolved count is asymmetric.* The nothink baseline is close to
  flip-free on all nine cells (`D_cf` between 0.002 and 0.032, Table 9), so almost the whole
  pair population sits in the agreeing state. Cell c draws on that large population; cell b
  can draw only on the 0.2–3.2% that was already flipping. The authors state this in §4.4
  and check it in Appendix H, Table 12, where an explicit independence model over the same
  two transition rates predicts `|c| > |b|` in all nine cells without any within-pair
  correlation. — evidence: **demonstrated**. This is a controlled calculation, it is
  published in the paper, and AXV re-derived it independently at zero cost (run
  `axv-2609.30768-asymmetry-01`); the direction holds in 9/9.
- *Why thinking is a confidence amplifier that sharpens both fair and biased outcomes.*
  The anchor/mover account, evidenced by `CDPG` trajectories rising in cells c and d and
  decaying in cells a and b (Figures 3, 4). — evidence: **asserted**, and partly circular.
  Cell c is *defined* as "the pair flipped under thinking", so averaging `|CDPG_K|` inside
  cell c and obtaining 0.835 is entailed by the definition rather than evidence for it. The
  part that is not circular is the depth profile and the anchor/mover split, and that rests
  on 54, 56 and **16** cell-c pairs (Table 6), with no spread, no interval and no seed
  reported on any trajectory in the paper.

**Alternative routes.**

1. **Report the rate ratio, not the count ratio.** Replace `|c| / |b|` with
   `p(agree → M) / p(M → agree)` — the per-pair rate at which thinking flips an agreeing
   pair, against the rate at which it returns an already-flipping pair. Mechanism: the count
   ratio is a product of a transition rate and a starting-population ratio, and only the
   first term is about thinking; dividing it out leaves the quantity the mechanism section
   actually describes. Pros: it is computable from Tables 4, 13 and 14 as already printed,
   so it costs nothing and needs no new inference; it is the number that stays inside one
   order of magnitude across cells, 0.022 to 0.168, where the count ratio spans 20x; and it
   is the stronger claim, because it is the one that is true in all nine cells rather than in
   the median. Cons: it throws away the operationally important half of the number — if the
   baseline barely flips, then thinking producing 105 new counterfactual disagreements is a
   real harm whether or not the rate ratio is flattering, and a reader given only `G` could
   conclude the problem is smaller than it is. A practitioner needs the created count; a
   mechanism claim needs the rate. **Why-not-chosen** (inference): the count ratio is what
   survives an McNemar test on paired binary outcomes, and the authors built a paired design
   around it. **Cost to test:** zero, arithmetic on published tables. **Would-it-win: yes**,
   and it is a better paper.

2. **Swap the non-thinking arm for a real one, and remove CDPG.** Two independent changes
   to the same design. First, Qwen3-32B ships a trained `/no_think` mode, and the paper's
   baseline is instead a pre-filled empty `<think></think>` block — a prompt hack, which the
   authors concede in §6 ("a small fraction of generations may still emit reasoning tokens
   after this prefix"). Second, drop the `CDPG` instrument entirely: the headline comes from
   Table 2, which is one greedy decode per arm, so it needs 45,000 decodes, not the stated
   900,000 forward passes. Pros: a trained non-thinking mode is a control that was designed
   for the purpose instead of inferred from a prefix; and the compute drop is 95%, which on
   AXV's budget is the difference between a feasible and an infeasible replication.
   Cons: the within-model pairing that makes the McNemar test valid is partly lost, because
   `/no_think` is a mode boundary the model was trained across rather than a single forward
   pass; and removing CDPG deletes contribution 3, the anchor/mover account, which is the
   only evidence that thinking is a *directional* sharpening rather than added noise — so
   the paper would keep the result and lose the explanation. **Why-not-chosen** (stated for
   CDPG): the paper is explicitly diagnostic and positions the instruments as the
   contribution. **Cost to test:** one 8B model, one dataset, both arms, well under an hour
   on the board-set instance. **Would-it-win: maybe** — the cheaper, more honest control is
   clearly better, but the paper's within-model pairing is a real design strength and
   `/no_think` is not obviously stronger than the block hack for these three models.

3. **A note on the test AXV proposed, which cannot answer the question as posed.** The
   brief asks to re-run the ablation on a counterfactual set where the two options differ
   only on an attribute the model has never seen varied, and to read a surviving ratio as
   "the finding is about reasoning". That inference does not hold. Swapping the attribute
   changes *what is being protected*, but it leaves the quantity that actually drives the
   ratio untouched: the nothink baseline will still be near flip-free on a weak attribute, so
   `F` stays large and `c/b` will likely stay large too. A surviving ratio would not show
   the effect is about reasoning; it would show the same near-degenerate baseline, and the
   experiment would produce a false positive for the reading the brief wants to avoid. The
   construct that has to vary is `D_cf_nothink` — the rate at which the *non-thinking* arm
   flips — and the cheapest way to vary it is not a new run at all: hold `G` fixed and read
   the ratio off the nine existing cells, where `D_cf_nothink` already spans 0.002 to 0.032.
   That is what run `axv-2609.30768-asymmetry-01` does, and the paper's own Table 12 did the
   stronger version of it, predicting the asymmetry from an independence model in which the
   two sides of a pair are uncorrelated and therefore share nothing of the pair structure
   that "thinking" is supposed to be acting on. **Cost to test:** zero for the arithmetic
   version. **Would-it-win: the question is already answered in the paper's own appendix**,
   and the answer is that the count ratio is not a property of thinking.

**Evidence quality.**

- **Seeds.** One. Greedy decoding (T=0) makes each flip a deterministic function of the
  trace, and the authors use this correctly to rule out *sampling* variance in the c-vs-b
  comparison. It does not rule out the variance that matters: quantisation, prompt
  template, and dataset sample are each fixed at one setting, so there is no way to
  separate a property of thinking from a property of this prompt under this quantisation.
  The 5,000 records are a single unreported sample of 48,842 on Adult.
- **Ablations.** Partial, and pointed at the wrong target. Appendix C sweeps the
  `CDPG` segmentation `K ∈ {5, 10, 20}` — but only on Qwen3-32B, only on the propagation
  *slope*, and the c/b ratio is invariant to `K` by construction, so this ablation cannot
  move the headline. Appendix D adds two off-lineage models at N=1,000, which is the most
  valuable check in the paper. There is **no** ablation of the empty-block baseline, **no**
  ablation of the natural-language template, and **no** contamination test.
- **Baselines.** The non-thinking arm is the right control and is stronger than anything in
  the prior literature. It is weak in one specific, acknowledged way: it is a prompt hack
  rather than a trained mode.
- **Compute.** 900,000 forward passes, 4-bit AWQ on A100 80 GB. Not disclosed: wall clock,
  dollar cost, or generated token count. Not disclosed and material: the fraction of created
  flips where the deciding side sat within, say, 0.1 of the 0.5 threshold under the nothink
  arm. The paper measures a *hard* decision on a *quantised* model, and never reports how
  many of the 3,508 created flips were near-threshold. That single number would bound how
  much of the effect is quantisation plus jitter.
- **Data.** The weakest dimension, and unaddressed. Adult, COMPAS and Credit are among the
  most widely reproduced tabular datasets in existence; a 32B model has near-certainly seen
  the UCI Adult CSV *with labels* in pretraining. Rendering to prose with a deterministic
  template blocks memorisation of the prose form, not of the record-to-label mapping. A
  memorising model produces near-deterministic answers that move only under attention, which
  is exactly the regime where this effect lives. No held-out or synthetic-split check is run.
- **Evaluations.** Three datasets, three models, plus two replication models; 41,404 paired
  records in the main study, 5,559 in Appendix D. All binary classification. All protected
  attributes rendered as one adjective plus, for the gendered two, pronoun substitution. All
  outcomes Yes/No. Nothing on multi-class attributes, nothing on free-form generation, no
  equalised odds, no calibration — the paper names all of these as out of scope.
- **The single most likely way this claim is wrong: the headline "roughly 5 times in all
  nine" is the pooled value of a ratio whose per-cell values span 2.4x to 48.3x, and 80% of
  the log-variance in that ratio comes from a non-thinking-baseline population that is a
  property of the three datasets, not of reasoning.** A reader who takes "5x" as a constant
  of thinking will be wrong in a specific, predictable way: on R1-Distill-Credit the same
  models produce 48x, and on Qwen3-COMPAS they produce 2.4x. The McNemar tests are decisive
  for `c ≠ b`, which is a much weaker claim than `c ≈ 5b`, and the paper's significance
  column tests the former while its headline asserts the latter. Note that the paper is not
  concealing this: Table 12 prints the per-cell ratios, §4.4 says the asymmetry is
  "a structural consequence of any non-trivial transition probability acting on an imbalanced
  nothink population", and Appendix H shows the empirical ratios are **5 to 100x smaller
  than** what that first-order account predicts. The mechanism section is the better
  contribution, and the headline is a number that does not survive contact with its own
  appendix.
- **Second-most-likely:** the Appendix D replication is weaker than its framing. Two of the
  six cells have `b = 0`, so their per-cell ratio is undefined, and the pooled 7.5x folds
  both in — worth only +0.3%, so not a real distortion, but the text's "at ratios
  (4.9x–7.8x)" describes four cells and reads as six. The paper does state the hedge
  ("in every setting with a non-trivial number of discordant pairs"), which is the right
  hedge, so this is a presentation point and not a defect.

**Confidence.** medium — the paper's own arithmetic is fully reproducible, and the claim it
is most often quoted for is arithmetically decidable from tables it publishes; but every
causal statement about the thinking process itself rests on one model family, one prompt
template, one quantisation, one dataset sample, and cell-c trajectory groups of 16 to 56
pairs with no reported spread, and the contamination risk on three memorised tabular
benchmarks is untested. AXV's own re-derivation is exact on every input, which raises
confidence in the *arithmetic* and says nothing about the *causal* claim.

Unknowns: whether the created/resolved rate ratio `G < 1` survives a prompt template that
makes the protected attribute decision-relevant rather than incidental — this is the single
experiment that would move this memo to high or low; whether the empty-`<think></think>`
baseline differs from a trained `/no_think` mode on these three models; how many of the 3,508
created flips were decided by a side within 0.1 of threshold, which bounds the quantisation
contribution; whether the effect survives on a tabular split the models cannot have
memorised; whether `asym(c)` reaching 0.53 on QwQ-COMPAS is a real Non-White tilt or an
artefact of the 1,700-pair `YY` stratum; whether the ~59x pooled `F` would be materially
different on a dataset where the protected attribute genuinely drives the label; and
whether the paper's released code at `github.com/pd90506/fairness_audit` reproduces Table 2
on a single GPU, which nobody has checked and which is the cheapest remaining test.

- **The decomposition, which is the point of the run.** Writing the count ratio as
  `F × G`, where `F = |agree|_nt / |M|_nt` is entirely a property of the **non-thinking**
  arm and `G = p(agree → M) / p(M → agree)` is the only term describing the **thinking**
  transition:

  | cell | N | b | c | c/b | F (nothink) | G (thinking) |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | Qwen3-Adult | 5000 | 75 | 362 | 4.83 | 45.7 | 0.106 |
  | Qwen3-COMPAS | 5000 | 143 | 344 | 2.41 | 33.5 | 0.072 |
  | Qwen3-Credit | 4996 | 23 | 105 | 4.57 | 207.2 | 0.022 |
  | QwQ-Adult | 5000 | 152 | 443 | 2.91 | 30.1 | 0.097 |
  | QwQ-COMPAS | 5000 | 15 | 351 | 23.40 | 332.3 | 0.070 |
  | QwQ-Credit | 4984 | 121 | 595 | 4.92 | 39.2 | 0.125 |
  | R1-Adult | 4999 | 77 | 646 | 8.39 | 50.0 | 0.168 |
  | R1-COMPAS | 1430 | 4 | 179 | 44.75 | 356.5 | 0.126 |
  | R1-Credit | 4995 | 10 | 483 | 48.30 | 498.5 | 0.097 |

  `F` spans 30.1 to 498.5, a **16.6x** spread, tracking the non-thinking counterfactual flip
  rate. `G` spans 0.022 to 0.168, a 7.6x spread, and is **below 1 in all nine cells**. The
  observed ratio spans 2.41 to 48.30, a 20.1x spread. Decomposing the log-variance:
  **80% comes from `F` and 20% from `G`**, with `pearson(ln F, ln c/b) = 0.872` and
  `spearman(F, c/b) = 0.867`. The count ratio is a baseline-population artefact wearing a
  reasoning costume. The genuine, consistent, nine-of-nine finding is the other half: **in
  every cell, thinking returns an already-flipping pair to agreement 6x to 45x more often
  per pair than it flips an agreeing pair.** That is a real property of the thinking
  transition, it is the paper's better claim, and it is not the number the abstract leads
  with. Pooled, `5.66 = 59.2 × 0.0956`.
